# Report 1 — Fundraising for a Deep-Tech VC Fund

**Best-in-class strategy, calibrated to a sub-$250M frontier-science manager (PLC Neurotech Fund I and successors)**

*Prepared 19 September 2026. Figures are sourced inline. Where a number comes from a single practitioner blog or a captive dataset rather than an audited industry source, it is flagged. Treat flagged figures as directional.*

---

## 0. The argument in one page

Fund fundraising is not a persuasion problem. It is a **search-and-matching problem run under a credibility constraint**, and it decomposes into four things that can each be systematized:

1. **Segmentation.** Most LPs cannot buy you, regardless of how good you are. A $150M neurotech Fund I is structurally uninvestable for a pension (too small to matter, too risky for the sleeve), structurally *ideal* for a science-motivated family office, and a coin flip for a specialist fund-of-funds. The first and largest source of wasted fundraising effort is talking to capital that could not have said yes.
2. **Sequencing.** The LP community is small, dense, and gossip-efficient. Reference leakage means a list can be burned once and never recovered. Best-in-class managers therefore sequence deliberately: refine on Tier 2/3, anchor with Tier 1, and never mass-blast.
3. **Coverage.** You need 2–4x your target in pipeline commitments to net your target, because soft circles decay. Coverage ratio discipline is the single highest-leverage forecasting concept in the whole process.
4. **Proof of edge that is inspectable.** LPs discount asserted edge to roughly zero. They credit edge that can be *toured, counted, or checked* — a lab, a media operation with measurable inbound, a fellowship with a placement rate, a company-creation platform with throughput. This is the place where Protocol Labs has an unusually strong and unusually under-packaged asset.

Everything below is an elaboration of those four, plus the market conditions that set the difficulty level and the operating cadence that executes them.

**The uncomfortable headline for 2026:** this is the hardest fundraising market for emerging managers in fifteen years, and the structural cause is not sentiment but arithmetic — LPs have received less cash back than they have paid in for four consecutive years, so there is simply less to recycle, and what remains is concentrating into the largest, most legible franchises.

## 1. The market you are actually raising into

The data is unambiguous and it is bimodal.

**Fund formation at the small end has collapsed.** In 2025, 585 US VC funds raised roughly $67B. First-time funds numbered just **101 — the lowest since 2011**, down 77.9% from 457 in 2021 ([NVCA 2026 Yearbook](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nvca-releases-2026-yearbook-charts-a-venture-industry-in-transition-302740013.html)). A parallel dataset puts total US funds raised at 1,793 in 2022 → 1,059 in 2024 → 626 in 2025, a 65% decline from peak, and first-time funds at 478 (2022) → 106 (2025), raising a combined $7.3B ([PitchBook–NVCA data, via analysis](https://dollarcommerce.substack.com/p/venture-capital-is-shrinking-heres-what-that-means-for-you)). The two first-time-fund counts (101 vs 106) differ by source methodology; the direction is identical.

**Capital is concentrating violently.** The top 10 funds of 2025 captured $22B — **32.9% of all VC capital raised**, up 2.5x from 13% in 2021. By Q1 2026, established firms captured **90.9%** of all fundraising, and funds over $1B represented **71.9%** of YTD 2026 capital. In H1 2026, three firms — a16z, Thrive, and Founders Fund — raised **48.1% of all capital** ([PitchBook Q2 2026 Venture Monitor](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q2-2026-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor)).

**The median fund is shrinking while the mean explodes.** Median fund size fell to $15.3M in Q1 2026 while the average jumped to $289.7M — the arithmetic signature of a barbell.

**The cause is DPI, not appetite.** LP contributions have exceeded distributions for **four consecutive years**. 2025 saw $217.1B in venture-backed exits across 1,463 deals, but 859 unicorns are collectively valued at $4.34T and only an estimated 30–40 exited that year. Enormous paper value, almost no cash. LPs re-up with managers who have returned money and defer everyone else.

**The release valve is secondaries.** 2025 secondary volume hit **$240B, up 48% YoY** — $125B LP-led, $115B GP-led — with average continuation-vehicle size around $900M and venture/growth GP-led pricing at 78% of NAV ([Jefferies 2025 Global Secondary Market Review](https://www.jefferies.com/insights/the-big-picture/2025-global-secondary-market-review-another-record-breaking-year/)). This matters to you twice: it is where some LPs are getting the liquidity that funds your commitment, and it is a liquidity story you can eventually offer your own LPs.

**Time-to-close has stretched.** Median fund close time reached roughly **15 months** — the longest in over a decade. Practitioner sources put first-time funds at 12–18 months as a base case, with real tail risk to 24 months absent an anchor ([VC Lab](https://govclab.com/2026/08/10/vc-statistics); [Decile Group](https://decilegroup.com/articles/vc-statistics)). Plan the raise as an 18-month campaign with a 12-month stretch goal, not the reverse.

**LP concentration per fund is rising.** Median LP count per fund roughly halved to 23 in 2025-vintage funds, while anchor-check size as a share of fund size rose past 22% (from ~19% historically) ([Carta Fund Economics Report 2025](https://carta.com/data/fund-economics-report-2025-full-article/) — note Carta's dataset is its own administered client base and skews small/emerging).

### What this implies strategically

- **Do not plan a broad raise.** The market rewards a small number of deep, high-conviction relationships. Twenty-three LPs, one of whom is 22%+ of the fund, is the modal outcome now.
- **The anchor is the whole ballgame.** Practitioner consensus is that 15–30% of target pre-committed before broad outreach is the threshold that unlocks everything else, and that a first close below ~25% of target reads as distress ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/the-most-common-fundraising-mistakes-emerging-fund-managers-make)).
- **Right-size the target.** A target disconnected from the strategy's return math is a first-order red flag. For a neurotech fund, the portfolio-construction model has to survive the question "how does this return 3x net given how capital-intensive and long-duration these companies are?" A smaller, defensible fund that closes beats a larger one that stalls at 40% and dies.
- **Duration is a feature you must price.** Standard 10-year fund life does not fit neurotech. Address term, extensions, recycling, and possible continuation-vehicle paths *in the LPA and in the deck*, proactively. LPs read an unaddressed duration mismatch as naivety.

## 2. LP segmentation — the capital map for a frontier-science fund

### 2.1 The structural filter

Before any qualitative fit assessment, three arithmetic filters eliminate most of the universe:

1. **Minimum viable check.** Most institutions cannot write below $5M and cannot be more than 10–20% of a fund. A $5M minimum against a 10% cap implies a $50M floor on fund size for that LP to participate at all. Run this in both directions for every prospect.
2. **The 100-LP rule.** Investment Company Act §3(c)(1) caps beneficial owners at 100 for most private funds; a "qualifying venture capital fund" exemption raises this to 250 for funds under $10M. In practice this forces $250K–$500K minimums to stay under the cap ([Samir Kaji](https://samirkaji.medium.com/the-100-lp-rule-is-a-problem-that-needs-solving-2b0921b008bf)). If your target is $150M and your average check is $2M, you need ~75 LPs — feasible but tight, and it argues hard for a few large anchors.
3. **Mandate eligibility.** Many institutions simply cannot buy a first-time fund, a sub-$100M fund, or a single-sector fund, by written policy. This is a yes/no field, not a persuasion target.

### 2.3 The specialist seeders — your highest-yield institutional class

These are the institutions whose *entire business* is underwriting managers like you. They are few, they are named, and they should be mapped exhaustively:

- **Sapphire Partners** (Beezer Clarkson) — the most visible institutional backer of new managers; took over CalSTRS' entire emerging-manager portfolio in 2023, making it CalSTRS' outsourced access point to this segment ([Sapphire](https://sapphireventures.com/press/calstrs-and-sapphire-partners-join-forces/)). Also runs *OpenLP*, which is the single best public corpus on how LPs think.
- **Cendana Capital** (Michael Kim) — closed $470M in 2023 specifically to back seed-stage fund managers globally ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/09/08/exclusive-cendana-capital-closes-on-470m-more-to-back-seed-stage-fund-managers)).
- **Screendoor** — explicitly underwrites first-time managers; Lisa Cawley is its first MD ([Hunter Walk](https://hunterwalk.medium.com/changing-the-face-s-of-venture-capital-screendoor-hires-lisa-cawley-our-first-managing-director-1afcd3685d1)).
- **Ahoy Capital** (Chris Douvos) — ~$540M AUM, a prominent voice in emerging-manager LP circles.
- **Recast Capital** — first-time managers, reportedly in the $50–150M fund-size band (secondary source; verify the mandate directly).
- **First Close Partners** — brands itself as "the fund that writes the first check into the first check."
- **GCM Grosvenor** — dedicated Small and Emerging Manager platform across asset classes.
- **Industry Ventures** — long-standing emerging-manager and secondaries player, acquired by Goldman Sachs Asset Management in 2025, now inside a much larger institutional platform.
- **Vintage Investment Partners**, **Plexo Capital**, **Allocate** (Samir Kaji) — adjacent access and education platforms.

**How to work this class:** they are the cheapest source of *calibration*, not just capital. Even a pass from Sapphire or Screendoor, if you get real feedback, is worth more than twenty polite family-office meetings. Sequence a few of them early — but not your single best-fit one, which you save until the narrative is tight.

**Caveat on state pension emerging-manager programs** (TRS Illinois, NY Common, LACERS, Connecticut CRPTF, NJ, MassPRIM): these mandates are real and large, but in practice they are deployed *through* fund-of-funds intermediaries rather than as direct checks to a $50M Fund I. Target the intermediary, not the pension.

### 2.4 The deep-tech-specific LP set

One analysis of the most active deep-tech LPs puts the composition at roughly **fund-of-funds 43%, family offices 23%, government-backed vehicles 17%, SWFs 7%, with asset managers/endowments/pensions at ~3% each**, and skewed toward Europe (~47%) over North America (~27%) ([AQVC Raise](https://aqvcraise.substack.com/p/deep-techs-30-most-active-lps) — the percentage breakdown is sourced; the underlying named list is paywalled and unverified).

Take two things from this. First, **83% of active deep-tech LP capital sits in three buckets** — FoFs, family offices, and government-backed vehicles — which is a dramatically narrower target set than a generalist fund faces. Second, **Europe is materially over-represented**, which argues for a genuinely international coverage plan rather than a Bay Area/NYC default.

The structural innovations that make deep tech underwritable for LPs who otherwise wouldn't touch it:

- **Blended / catalytic capital.** Prime Coalition's **Azolla Ventures** raised a $239M vehicle blending foundation PRI dollars (which can be concessionary by design) with market-rate LP capital, explicitly to fund bets pure commercial capital won't underwrite alone ([ImpactAlpha](https://impactalpha.com/prime-coalitions-azolla-ventures-raises-239-million-to-catalyze-climate-capital/); [Prime Coalition](https://www.primecoalition.org/press/azolla-ventures-launch-press-release)). This is a *capital-stack* innovation, not a branding one, and it is the most directly transferable structure to a neurotech fund. You already have this on your radar; Report 3 works it in detail.
- **Institutional anchor + long duration.** The Engine model: a university anchor that simultaneously supplies the LP base and the technical-diligence pipeline.
- **Mission-motivated individuals.** In neurotech specifically, this is the most under-mapped and highest-conviction segment: people whose families have Parkinson's, ALS, epilepsy, depression, or paralysis, and people with a longevity/human-enhancement thesis. Their decision function is not risk-adjusted return. Your Bryan Johnson pitch is an instance of this pattern; the pattern deserves a systematic list, not one-offs.

### 2.5 A scoring model for prioritization

Two independent practitioner frameworks converge on the same structure. CRV's public methodology (written for founders targeting VCs, but the logic transfers directly) applies three sequential filters — does the prospect actually *lead*, does the stated thesis match the actual portfolio (and are there conflicts), and does the check-size math work — then sorts into three tiers, with the explicit tactic of running a lower-priority first wave to refine the pitch before burning Tier 1 ([CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/how-to-build-investor-target-list)). VC Lab's LP-matching framework scores across five dimensions — check-size fit, actual capital availability *right now*, fund-size alignment, realistic track-record expectations, and relationship depth — and attaches cadence discipline: warm prospects get a touchpoint every 2–3 weeks ([VC Lab](https://govclab.com/2026/04/07/limited-partner-matching/)).

Synthesized into a scoring model you could actually encode:

**Hard gates (binary — fail any and the prospect is out):**
- Can write a check ≥ your minimum and ≤ your concentration cap
- Mandate permits first-time / sub-$X / single-sector funds
- No disqualifying conflict (competing fund in portfolio, or a competing direct position)
- Has dry powder in this vintage (not "invests in venture" but "is deploying now")

**Weighted score (0–5 each):**
- **Thesis fit** — do they have a stated or revealed interest in frontier science / health / long-duration tech? (weight 3)
- **Relationship depth** — cold / one degree / warm intro available / existing relationship (weight 3)
- **Decision speed** — principal-decided vs committee vs consultant-gated (weight 2)
- **Signal value** — does their name unlock others? (weight 2)
- **Strategic value beyond capital** — deal flow, scientific network, follow-on capital, LP referrals (weight 2)
- **Concession tolerance** — can they take a catalytic/first-loss/concessionary position? (weight 1, but high option value)

**Tiering:** Tier 1 = passes gates, score ≥ 70th percentile, warm path confirmed. Tier 2 = passes gates, score mid, path uncertain. Tier 3 = opportunistic.

**Sequencing rule:** first wave = Tier 2 (refine the pitch, gather objections, build the FAQ). Second wave = Tier 1 anchors. Third wave = Tier 3 and the long tail. Never invert this.

## 3. Narrative — what a fundable thesis asserts

### 3.1 The structural pattern

Across the durable franchises, a fundable thesis is a **falsifiable structural claim about a change in the world**, not a sector preference. It has four load-bearing parts:

1. **A named inefficiency.** USV's Thesis 3.0 names *access* — "USV backs trusted brands that broaden access to knowledge, capital, and well-being by leveraging networks, platforms, and protocols" ([USV](https://www.usv.com/writing/2018/04/usv-thesis-3-0/)). Founders Fund named *capital mispricing of hard things* — "we wanted flying cars, instead we got 140 characters." The Engine names *tough tech* and explicitly reframes technical risk as the *source* of edge rather than a liability ([Tough Tech](https://engine-ventures.files.svdcdn.com/production/Reports-and-Publications/Tough-Tech-Publication-02.pdf)).
2. **A why-now trigger** tied to a real discontinuity, not a vibe.
3. **A why-us mechanism that is structural** — a network, a facility, a research program, a platform — rather than personal ("we're smart, we're connected").
4. **A cadence of public restatement.** USV has version-numbered its thesis three times over fifteen years. DCVC publishes an annual Deep Tech Opportunities Report ([DCVC](https://www.dcvc.com/reports/deep-tech-opportunities-report-2025/)). This lets an LP observe your reasoning *updating* over time rather than take a one-shot pitch on faith. It is the single most underused credibility device available to a manager with a strong intellectual output — and you already produce this output.

### 3.2 The neurotech-specific framing problem

Your LPs will not be able to diligence the science. That is the core constraint, and the standard substitutes are:

- **A scientific advisory board that has actually reviewed specific deals** — named, credible, and demonstrably engaged, not decorative.
- **Milestone-gated capital deployment language.** Translate "decades of science" into discrete de-risking gates: works in vivo → chronic stability → human safety → efficacy → reimbursement pathway. Show how your check sizes and reserves map onto those gates. This is the single move that converts "ambitious" into "underwritten" in an allocator's mind.
- **Comparables and financing-progression evidence.** The sector now has a real capital-markets record: Neuralink's reported ~$650M Series E at a ~$9B+ valuation; **Synchron's $200M Series D** in November 2025 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251106150841/en/Synchron-Raises-$200-Million-Series-D-to-Advance-Brain-Computer-Interface-Technology)); Precision Neuroscience's ~$102M raise; **Axoft's $55M Series A** in April 2026 targeting FDA approval in 2027 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260429285106/en/Axoft-Secures-$55M-Series-A-to-Advance-Clinical-Trials-Globally-for-its-Bio-Inspired-Implantable-Brain-Computer-Interface)); Motif Neurotech's $18.75M Series A; Paradromics, seeded at $20M by Prime Movers Lab; Sanmai's ~$12M with Reid Hoffman backing a non-invasive ultrasound path. Combined disclosed BCI-sector capitalization has been put above **$15B**, with capital concentrating into a small group of firms ([Neurofounders](https://www.neurofounders.co/articles/bci-enters-a-new-funding-stage)).
- **Honest exit framing.** This is where most neurotech decks lose credibility. **NeuroPace's 2021 Nasdaq IPO (NPCE) is essentially the only clean public-market comparable** in the sector. Do not paper over that. Frame returns via (a) staged step-up financings as realized evidence of technical progress, (b) strategic acquisition optionality from medtech incumbents once clinical and regulatory risk is retired, and (c) explicit acknowledgment that this needs a 12–15 year horizon with term extensions, recycling, and possibly a continuation vehicle. An LP who hears you name the exit problem first will trust the rest of the deck. An LP who has to raise it themselves will discount everything.
- **The "multiply by pi" norm.** Some deep-tech funds explicitly pre-empt the timeline objection by stating a multiplier on founder estimates. Naming your own adjustment factor, and showing that your reserve model uses it, is disarming.

Note the market-structure argument embedded in the $15B/concentration figure: it is a barbell. Your thesis must explain **why you back a likely winner rather than the median company** — because the median company in this sector will not return capital.

### 3.3 Proof of edge — making it inspectable

LPs discount asserted edge. They credit edge that is *falsifiable and observable*: a media operation's inbound can be counted; a lab can be toured; a fellowship's placement rate can be checked; a company-creation platform's throughput can be counted. The paradigm cases:

- **a16z's media and talent operation** — reported as roughly a ten-person media team running podcasts, newsletters, and partner brands, functioning as a pipeline: content attracts founders → founders join the ecosystem → ecosystem supplies talent → talent scales companies ([analysis](https://refiningventure.beehiiv.com/p/a16z-s-360m-year-media-operation); specific spend and headcount figures are third-party estimates, unverified against a16z disclosures).
- **SOSV's IndieBio and HAX** — wet labs and prototyping shops that generate proprietary pre-seed flow because the company is often incorporated *inside* the accelerator ([SOSV](https://sosv.com/sosv-2024-spotlight-on-deep-tech/)).
- **The Engine's facility** — shared lab space and equipment that lets the fund *observe experiments directly*, which is a diligence advantage no generalist can replicate.
- **Deep Science Ventures** — inverts sourcing entirely: builds the IP and thesis in-house, recruits founders afterward. DSV's own framing claims roughly 100x the company-creation efficiency of elite university tech transfer (DSV's own figure, unverified as an independent estimate) ([coverage](https://www.agnavigator.com/Article/2026/09/01/deep-science-ventures-the-investor-starting-with-problems-not-technologies/)).
- **Activate Fellowship** — pays scientist-entrepreneurs to spend two years de-risking a technology before committing to a company ([Activate](https://activate.org/the-fellowship)), effectively subsidizing the risk-retirement phase VCs otherwise fund with dilutive capital. Funds with warm relationships here get first look at founders who have already survived technical validation.
- **Bloomberg Beta's open-sourced manual on GitHub** ([repo](https://github.com/Bloomberg-Beta/Manual)) — publishes check sizes, diligence process, and the explicit statement that it invests independently of its anchor LP's interests. It converts an LP diligence question into a pre-answered, verifiable artifact.

### 3.4 Your specific edge, and why it is currently under-packaged

Protocol Labs has an asset structure that maps directly onto what LPs credit, and which almost no sub-$250M manager can claim:

- **A research program (PL Neuro) that is upstream of the fund.** This is The Engine's model and DSV's model combined — you don't just see deals, you help originate the science. That is the strongest form of sourcing edge there is, *if you can evidence it*: number of projects, researchers engaged, spinouts formed or in formation.
- **A convening engine.** LabWeek, Funding the Commons, and the PL ecosystem events ([Protocol Labs](https://www.protocol.ai/blog/labweek-public-goods); [Funding the Commons](https://research.protocol.ai/blog/2022/funding-the-commons/)) are the same mechanism that makes Capital Allocators, Colossus, Foresight's Vision Weekends, and Edge City work: convening compresses a multi-year trust-building cycle into a few intense days and produces a *verifiable* relationship graph — who was in the room, who co-hosted. Your podcast is the same asset in serial form.
- **A talent and network graph** across distributed systems, cryptography, and now neuro, with unusual reach into a crypto-wealth UHNW population that most neurotech managers cannot access at all.
- **Operator credibility.** You build and run systems yourself. For a technical-risk fund, that is an underwriting argument, not a bio line.

**The packaging gap.** You have already diagnosed a brand-voice mismatch between founder-facing and LP-facing communications. That is exactly the failure mode the practitioner literature describes: the founder-facing voice is built for differentiation among founders and does not automatically translate into LP-credible differentiation, which requires *evidence* (attribution data, realized markups, named platform assets with counted throughput) rather than tone. The resolution used by the best firms is not two separate stories — it is one intellectually honest story where the public thesis work serves both audiences, with the harder quantified case carried in the data room and quarterly letters. USV is the canonical example: the same document works for founders and allocators because it is honest rather than tailored.

Where firms fail at this: promising founders conviction and speed while telling LPs rigorous diversified process; or making maximalist public claims that read as ideological rather than risk-managed to an allocator who must justify the commitment to their own investment committee. For a deep-tech manager the specific trap is that the founder narrative can be maximally ambitious while the LP narrative must be milestone-gated — and if you don't do that translation explicitly, the allocator reads ambition as an absence of underwriting discipline.

**Concrete recommendation:** publish a version-numbered fund thesis, annually. Make it a real artifact — dated, specific, falsifiable, with what you got wrong last year. It does triple duty: founder-facing positioning, LP-facing credibility, and a forcing function on your own thinking. It is also the cheapest possible substitute for a track record you don't yet have at fund scale.

### 4.2 LP deck anatomy

A synthesized best-in-class structure ([PipelineRoad](https://pipelineroad.com/blog/lp-pitch-deck-framework)):

1. Title / positioning — a concrete strategy statement, not a tagline
2–3. Strategy and investment criteria — sector, geography, stage, check size, ownership target
4–5. Market opportunity — with at least one *proprietary* data insight, not a market-size slide anyone could build
6–7. Sourcing and investment process — including funnel metrics: deals seen, deals diligenced, deals done
8. Value creation / platform — the inspectable asset
9–11. Track record — fund-level and deal-level, realized clearly separated from unrealized
12. Portfolio construction — count, check size range, concentration limits, reserve strategy
13. Current pipeline — 3–5 live opportunities as proof the thesis executes
14–15. Team — emphasizing prior working history together
16. Terms
17–18. Appendix — bios, case studies, references, policies

**Neurotech-specific additions:** a technical-risk-gating slide (the milestone ladder), a duration-and-liquidity slide (term, extensions, recycling, secondaries path), and a scientific advisory slide. Put the duration slide *early*, not in the appendix.

### 4.3 Track record when you don't have fund-level track record

The metrics LPs expect: IRR, TVPI, DPI, MOIC, plus loss ratio and PME benchmarking against a public index ([Carta](https://carta.com/learn/private-funds/management/track-record/)). The credibility mechanism for prior-firm or angel track record is **deal-by-deal attribution**: entry valuation, your specific role in sourcing and decision, follow-on participation, exit outcome — ideally corroborated in writing by the prior firm. Formal attribution letters are a recognized diligence artifact though not universally standardized.

Two rules that are close to absolute:
- **Never present gross deal returns without net fund-level context.** It is the most common instant red flag.
- **Angel track record without documented entry valuations and follow-on history is treated as unverifiable**, which is worse than not claiming it.

For PLC Neurotech, the honest track record story is probably composite: PL's own investing history, your personal angel positions, the fund's warehoused deals if any, and — critically — *the research program's output as a leading indicator*. Frame the last one as evidence of sourcing edge, not as investment performance. Conflating them is the failure mode.

### 4.4 Data room contents

Synthesized from institutional checklists ([VC Lab](https://govclab.com/2025/08/13/vc-data-room/)):

- **Legal/compliance** — PPM, regulatory filings, compliance policy, CCO contact, fund rules
- **Financials** — prior fund performance by vintage, sample LP quarterly/annual reports, capital call and distribution history
- **Fund model** — financial model with base/conservative/optimistic scenarios, portfolio construction, diversification, fee-and-carry waterfall, **valuation policy**
- **Portfolio** — past deal performance, warehoused deals, sample investment memos, current metrics
- **Research** — competitive landscape, sector deep dives, macro thesis support *(this is your strongest section — most emerging managers have nothing here)*
- **Team** — bios, references, per-person attribution, IC structure, succession
- **Presentation** — deck, one-pager, market summary, track record highlights

Build it before the raise starts. An LP who asks for something you don't have loses a week of momentum and gains a doubt.

### 4.5 Terms, and the structural traps you have already found

Norms from Carta's 2025 dataset (skews toward smaller/emerging managers administered on Carta): **2% management fee has been the median from 2018–2025** with a 75th percentile at 2.5%; **20% carry is standard**, with sub-$10M funds showing a 10th-percentile carry as low as 15% and $100M+ funds showing a 90th-percentile carry of 25–30%. **Hurdles are rare in venture** — only 9.5% of $1M–$10M funds and 12.4% of $100M+ funds carry a preferred return ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/fund-economics-report-2025-full-article/)).

GP commit: typically 1–3% of fund. Samir Kaji's argument — which is worth internalizing because sophisticated LPs share it — is that the nominal percentage matters less than percentage of personal net worth at risk, opportunity cost foregone, and cash-flow reality ([Kaji](https://samirkaji.medium.com/how-lps-should-really-think-about-gp-commits-in-emerging-managers-d7c43cfdbe47)).

You have already identified structural issues in the Fund I LPA around the clawback (§10.3), circular catch-up mechanics, and tax distribution leakage. Two general points worth stating anyway, because they matter for the *next* vehicle and for the tool:

- **Side letters and MFN are a systems problem, not a legal one.** Once you have a dozen LPs with bespoke terms, you need a machine-readable register of who got what, what the MFN carve-outs are, and which elections are open. This is a real data-model requirement for the tool.
- **Duration and term extension deserve pre-negotiation, not a later amendment.** For neurotech, a standard 10+1+1 is likely wrong. Negotiating it once, upfront, with the anchor is far cheaper than amending later.
- **A purpose-clause amendment for one LP** (as you drafted) is a precedent-setting event — it is exactly the kind of thing a targeting system should flag as affecting every future LP conversation via MFN.

### 4.6 The quarterly update is a fundraising instrument

Treated across practitioner sources as functionally part of the *next* raise. A consistent update cadence is one of the informal signals LPs use to judge operational discipline, and existing LPs who receive good updates are the highest-conversion referral channel you have. Start the cadence before you have a portfolio — an update on thesis, pipeline, and research program output is still an update.

## 5. Process mechanics

### 5.1 The funnel, with real numbers

Practitioner data, with appropriate caution about sample skew:

- **VC Lab's aggregate** across 950+ firms suggests **300+ LP prospects to secure ~50 commitments**, and identifies that funds which ultimately closed had gathered **~1.9x more soft commitments in their first six months** than funds that stalled ([VC Lab](https://govclab.com/2025/05/01/the-perfect-vc-fundraising-schedule/); [Decile](https://decilegroup.com/articles/vc-statistics)). Note this cohort skews very small — ~90% of its LP commitments went to funds targeting under $15M, average LP check $159K.
- **LPs typically require 3–7 touchpoints over 6–18 months** before committing; a curated 30–50 target list materially outperforms a 200+ blast, where conversion falls below 0.5% ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/the-most-common-fundraising-mistakes-emerging-fund-managers-make)).
- **Case studies bracket the range.** Homebrew's Fund I: ~40 warm-intro emails → ~20 meetings → 10 multi-conversation relationships → 5 yeses, with four institutional LPs supplying ~92% of the fund ([Hunter Walk](https://hunterwalk.com/2013/07/22/homebrew-100-days-of-fundraising/)). Hustle Fund's Elizabeth Yin: 345 personal meetings over 10 months to close $11.5M in $25K–$300K checks ([Yin](https://elizabethyin.com/2018/12/19/how-i-raised-my-11-5m-vc-fund/)).

The two case studies are the two ends of a strategic choice: **few large institutional checks** (Homebrew) or **many small relationship checks** (Hustle Fund). For a neurotech fund with a PL-scale network, the right answer is probably a hybrid — 2–3 anchors carrying 40–50%, a specialist FoF or two for institutional validation, and a long tail of mission-motivated individuals drawn from your existing graph. That hybrid is exactly what a targeting tool should be built to run, because it requires managing two very different cadences simultaneously.

### 5.2 Coverage ratios — the single most useful forecasting concept

VC Lab's framework distinguishes **soft-circled** LPs (interested, non-binding, 10–25% close probability) from **hard-circled** (signed commitment letter, 50–75%). The worked example: to net $2M in a developed LP market at 75%/25% close rates, you need $4M of pipeline commitments — **2x coverage**. In a harder market at 50%/10%, the same $2M needs $8M — **4x coverage** ([VC Lab](https://govclab.com/2023/05/05/hard-and-soft-commitments-for-vc/)).

2026 is a hard market. **Plan on 3–4x coverage.** For a $150M target, that means cultivating $450–600M of expressed interest. Stated that way, the scale of the required pipeline becomes obvious — and so does why a tool is worth building.

No source provides a canonical stage-probability schema beyond the soft/hard split, so the defensible model to encode is:

| Stage | Probability |
|---|---|
| Identified (qualified, not contacted) | 2% |
| First meeting held | 5% |
| Second meeting / materials reviewed | 10% |
| In diligence / data room access | 25% |
| Soft circle (verbal, no doc) | 15–25% |
| Hard circle (signed commitment letter) | 50–75% |
| Subscription docs out | 90% |
| Wired | 100% |

Note the deliberate non-monotonicity: **a soft circle is often worth less than active diligence**, because a verbal yes without process engagement is frequently politeness. Encode that.

### 5.3 Cadence and hygiene

- **Weekly operating rhythm** (VC Lab's prescription, adapt to your scale): 30–50 new prospects identified, 30–50 outreach touches, 15–20 LP meetings, 2–3 events attended or hosted.
- **24-hour follow-up rule** after every meeting. (VC Lab claims >40% improvement in next-step conversion; single-source, treat as directional but the practice is free.)
- **Decay rule:** after three meetings with no specific number discussed, move the LP off active pipeline to a passive nurture list. This is the discipline that prevents a pipeline from becoming a fiction.
- **Warm-prospect cadence:** a touchpoint every 2–3 weeks, not batch-and-wait.
- **Qualification SLA:** qualify or disqualify a new prospect within 90 days.

### 5.4 Tooling — and the gap

The tools split into two unrelated categories that get confused:

**Relationship/pipeline CRMs:** Affinity, Attio, 4Degrees, DealCloud (relationship-graph strength, but default to a B2B sales pipeline with no native concept of a soft commitment); Airtable and Notion (flexible, cheap, what most solo GPs actually use); Visible.vc (combines LP pipeline with portfolio monitoring and updates); Salesforce/HubSpot (over-general); Decile Hub (purpose-built for LP stages: outreach → intro meeting → commitment letter → LPA executed → wire received).

**Closing and fund-ops platforms:** Carta, AngelList, Sydecar, Passthrough, Anduin, Juniper Square, Allocate, Vauban/Odin. Indicative annual cost for a $10M fund: Sydecar ~$20K, Carta ~$30K, AngelList ~$40K, with AngelList most turnkey and Carta positioned for $10–30M+ funds ([comparison](https://teel.substack.com/p/the-emerging-vc-stack-angellist-vs) — single practitioner source, verify current pricing directly).

**The structural gaps that matter for what you're building:**
1. No general CRM models **non-binding soft commitments** or probability-weighted coverage natively.
2. All of them treat a signed subscription document as pipeline completion, when signature-to-wire is its own workflow.
3. None model that **an LP relationship persists across funds and vehicles** — the relationship is the durable object, the commitment is an event on it.
4. None model **a funder who can be an LP, a grantor, a co-investor, and an advisor simultaneously** — which is the whole point of Report 3.
5. None encode **MFN/side-letter state** as structured, queryable data.
6. None encode **concentration and eligibility constraints** as hard gates that auto-disqualify prospects.

That list is close to a product spec.

## 6. Diligence — what they actually check

Institutional LPs, usually via the ILPA DDQ:

- **Team** — experience, attribution, decision cohesion, prior working history, **key-person risk and succession**
- **Sourcing** — how you see what you see, evidenced with funnel data
- **Decision process** — IC structure, who can veto, how disagreement resolves
- **Portfolio construction** — discipline, reserves, concentration limits
- **Back office** — fund admin, audit, valuation policy, cash management, reporting
- **Operational due diligence (ODD)** — governance, AML/KYC, investor onboarding, financial reporting and NAV methodology, cybersecurity and technology infrastructure (fragmented spreadsheets vs centralized systems is read as a maturity signal), and oversight of outsourced providers ([Carta ODD](https://carta.com/learn/private-funds/management/fund-administration/operational-due-diligence/))
- **References** — existing LPs, portfolio founders, **and co-investors**. All three. Co-investor references are the ones emerging managers forget to prepare.
- **Background checks and reputational screening** on the GP and key team

For a fund embedded in a larger network like Protocol Labs, two extra questions will come, and you should pre-answer both:
- **Independence.** Does the fund invest on its merits, or is it a captive vehicle for the parent's interests? Bloomberg Beta's solution — publish the independence statement and the governance that backs it — is directly applicable.
- **Allocation and conflicts.** When PL Neuro's research produces a spinout, who invests, on what terms, and who decides? A written allocation policy pre-empts this. Without one, it is the single most likely diligence stall.

## 7. Failure modes

Two population-level statistics, both from single practitioner sources rather than audited datasets: **"60%+ of first-time VC fund managers never close Fund I"** ([ValueAddVC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/the-most-common-fundraising-mistakes-emerging-fund-managers-make)), and **"70% of fund managers do not raise a second fund,"** from a practitioner reporting conversations with ~1,200 managers over 24 months, who identifies the number-one 2024–26 failure driver as **the absence of a written, monthly-reconciled management-company P&L** ([Adam Metz](https://metz.substack.com/p/the-black-box-why-raises-fail)). That last point is worth pausing on: the modal failure is not a bad thesis, it is the GP running out of personal runway or losing LP confidence over their own firm's financial discipline.

The taxonomy:

1. **No anchor strategy.** Going broad without 15–30% pre-committed removes the social proof that unlocks everyone else.
2. **Unfocused targeting.** 200+ blasted contacts, <0.5% conversion, and a burned list.
3. **Generic thesis.** Positioning indistinguishable from incumbents that fails to answer why a founder chooses you.
4. **Startup-fundraising urgency applied to LPs.** LP cycles are 3–7 touchpoints over 6–18 months and resist compression. Pressure reads as desperation.
5. **Mismanaged close sequencing.** A first close under 25% of target signals distress and removes the deadline pressure that drives later closes.
6. **Unverifiable track record.** Angel claims without documented entry valuations and follow-on history.
7. **Oversized or mis-specified target.** A target disconnected from the strategy's return math.
8. **Reference leakage and signaling.** The LP community is small and interconnected. A lukewarm reference, or news of a stalled raise, circulates fast. This is why sequencing matters more here than in almost any other kind of fundraising.
9. **Management-company financial indiscipline.** Per the above, empirically the largest killer.

Add two that are specific to your situation:

10. **Voice mismatch.** The founder-facing brand and the LP-facing case reading as two different firms. You have diagnosed this; fixing it is a prerequisite to the raise, not a parallel workstream.
11. **Parent-network conflict ambiguity.** Unresolved questions about independence, allocation, and conflicts between PL, PL Neuro, and the fund. Solve on paper before the first institutional meeting.

## 8. A 12–18 month operating plan

**Phase 0 — Readiness (months −3 to 0, before any Tier 1 meeting)**
- Fix the voice problem: one coherent story, two registers.
- Publish thesis v1.0 as a dated, version-numbered public artifact.
- Build the full data room including valuation policy, allocation policy, and independence statement.
- Complete the ILPA DDQ cold.
- Write the portfolio construction model with explicit milestone-gating and duration assumptions.
- Assemble the track record attribution schedule with documentation.
- Stand up the pipeline system with stages, probabilities, and hard gates encoded.
- Build the target list: aim for 300–400 qualified prospects across the segments in §2, tiered.

**Phase 1 — Calibration (months 1–3)**
- Run 30–50 Tier 2 meetings. Explicit goal: collect objections, not commitments.
- Maintain an objection log. Every distinct objection gets a written answer that goes into the FAQ and, where warranted, into the deck.
- Approach 2–3 specialist FoFs early for feedback (not your single best-fit one).
- Target: thesis and deck stable by end of month 3.

**Phase 2 — Anchor (months 3–8)**
- Tier 1 outreach, anchor-first. Target 15–30% of fund committed.
- Negotiate the anchor's side letter knowing it sets MFN precedent for everyone after.
- Simultaneously cultivate the mission-motivated individual segment, which runs on a completely different (faster, conviction-driven) clock.

**Phase 3 — First close (months 8–12)**
- Target first close at ≥ 30% of target. Do not close below 25%.
- Use the close as a forcing event for soft circles. A dated close is the only real deadline in LP fundraising.

**Phase 4 — Momentum to final (months 12–18)**
- Rolling closes.
- Begin quarterly LP updates immediately after first close — they are the Fund II raise starting early.
- Track coverage ratio weekly against remaining target.

**Throughout:** 2–3 convenings or high-quality events per quarter. This is not networking overhead; per the research it is the mechanism that compresses a multi-year trust cycle into days, and it is the thing you are already unusually good at.

## 9. What this implies for the tool

The VC-side requirements, stated as a spec:

**Entities and relationships**
- LP entity ≠ LP relationship ≠ commitment. Model all three separately; the relationship is durable across funds.
- A funder family (e.g., a principal) may map to multiple legal entities with different tax status, mandates, and decision rights. Model the family tree.
- People are first-class: the decision-maker, the analyst, the gatekeeper, the advocate. Your existing PL-network event-log architecture with people as primary entities is the right instinct.

**Hard gates (auto-disqualify, surfaced as reasons)**
- Check size range vs fund minimum and concentration cap
- Mandate eligibility (first-time fund, fund size floor, single-sector)
- Vintage/dry-powder status
- Conflict detection against portfolio and competing funds

**Scoring and tiering**
- Weighted multi-dimensional score as in §2.5, with explainable component contributions
- Auto-tiering with manual override, and an audit trail of overrides

**Pipeline and forecasting**
- Stage model with the probabilities in §5.2, including the soft-circle/diligence non-monotonicity
- Coverage-ratio dashboard: committed vs weighted pipeline vs target, with time-to-close projection
- Decay rules and re-engagement triggers as first-class automation, not reminders

**The relationship graph**
- Warm-path computation: shortest and strongest introduction paths, with path quality scoring (an existing LP > a co-investor > a service provider > a conference acquaintance)
- Referral attribution: which relationships actually produced commitments, so the graph learns

**Materials and compliance state**
- Versioned materials with per-LP delivery tracking (who has which version)
- DDQ answer library with reuse
- **Side-letter and MFN register as structured data** — terms granted, carve-outs, open elections
- Data-room access log

**Event log**
- Append-only, replayable. Every touch, every objection, every version sent. This is what makes backtesting possible: which sequencing actually worked, which objections predicted a pass, which channels converted.

**The objection ontology** deserves its own mention. If you encode objections as a taxonomy rather than free text — "too early," "sector too narrow," "duration too long," "team unproven," "no DPI," "wrong vintage," "concentration" — you get an early-warning system: three LPs raising duration in one week means the deck needs a fix, not three more meetings.
