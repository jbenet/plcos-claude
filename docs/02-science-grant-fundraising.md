# Report 2 — Fundraising for Science & Technology Grant Programs

**Best-in-class strategy for raising philanthropic and non-dilutive capital, calibrated to a frontier R&D network (Protocol Labs R&D, PL Neuro, and FRO-style programs)**

*Prepared 19 September 2026. Sourced inline; single-source and unverified figures are flagged. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.*

---

## 0. The argument in one page

Five claims, which the rest of the report supports:

1. **Most serious science philanthropy is sourced, not applied for.** Moore and Kavli say outright that they do not accept unsolicited proposals. Packard, Searle, Klingenstein-Simons, Burroughs Wellcome, and Beckman are institution-nomination-only. Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy) states that its team "typically identifies most of our grantees directly." A conservative read of the funders surveyed here is that **a minority — plausibly a third or less — of major private science-philanthropy dollars is reachable through cold, unsolicited application.** The real work is getting discovered and pre-vetted by a program officer before any proposal exists.

2. **The funder's internal pipeline matters more than yours.** Coefficient Giving publishes its five stages: Strong Interest → Investigation → Conditional Approval → Formal Approval → Evaluation. Critically, it notes that at formal approval "nearly every grant that reaches this stage is approved and paid out." **The decision gate is stages 1–3 — inside the program officer's head and their internal memo — not the board vote.** Every tactic should be aimed at those three stages.

3. **The proposal is co-authored, not submitted.** In the Coefficient Giving model, when leadership wants substantial revisions the investigator loops the grantee back in. Sophisticated grant capital is won by becoming a collaborator on the program officer's internal case, not by writing a better document in isolation.

4. **Your organizational form is your biggest structural lever.** Most large science philanthropy cannot easily fund a for-profit R&D arm, and most of the newest, fastest-moving funders (Astera, Renaissance Philanthropy, Convergent Research, Speculative Technologies, Schmidt Sciences) are specifically built to fund *program theses*, not projects. The highest-yield move for PL R&D is probably not "apply for grants" — it is **to be constituted and positioned as the kind of vehicle these new intermediaries were designed to fund.**

5. **Senior FTE-hours, not dollars, are the binding constraint** — a conclusion you have already reached about PL R&D's cost structure. That conclusion should drive the entire strategy: it argues for a small number of very large, long-duration, low-overhead relationships, against a wide portfolio of small open-call applications, and *for* aggressive automation of everything upstream of the human conversation.

## 1. The funder landscape

### 1.1 Large private science funders — access mechanics

The practical question for each is not "what do they fund" but **"can I get in at all, and how."** Sorted by that:

**Genuinely open-call (apply directly):**

- **HHMI Investigators** — unusual among elite funders in being a real open competition. Next cycle opens 28 Sept 2027, closes 7 Mar 2028, roughly every five years. Requires tenured/tenure-track faculty with 7+ years running an independent lab and an active nationally competitive grant. Selectees become HHMI employees on a 7-year renewable term. A 2025 round funded 26 investigators with over $300M committed ([HHMI](https://www.hhmi.org/programs/investigators); [release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1051398)). **Not accessible to a non-academic R&D org** — worth knowing as a recruiting/affiliation lever, not a funding channel.
- **W.M. Keck Foundation** — one of the few large funders with a transparent non-LOI process. Institutions submit 6-page applications through a single named liaison officer, up to 3 per cycle, twice yearly (June/December). Typical awards **$1M–$1.3M over 3 years, with no indirect costs allowed**, explicitly targeting high-risk basic science and excluding applied/translational work ([Keck](https://www.wmkeck.org/research-overview/)). The no-indirects rule matters: it is a real cost to a lean org.
- **CZI** — runs a portfolio of open competitive RFAs rather than one program: Essential Open Source Software for Science (EOSS), Diversity & Inclusion, Education & Capacity Building, Communities of Practice, Sharing Platforms ([CZI](https://chanzuckerberg.com/science/programs-resources/open-science/grants/)). EOSS awards have run roughly **$50K–$260K per project**, with cycles totaling $3.8M–$16M. EOSS Cycle 6 is co-funded with Wellcome and Kavli — note the multi-funder pooling pattern. **EOSS is a direct fit for PL's open-source scientific infrastructure work** and one of the cleanest entry points in this whole landscape.
- **Wellcome Discovery Awards** — averaging **£3.5M over ~7 years** (up to 8), with requests over £5M drawing extra scrutiny. Two structural changes matter: from **23 Sept 2026 the scheme goes "always open"** with rolling review twice yearly; and from **29 Oct 2026 eligibility narrows to the UK plus LMICs in Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia** ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/research-funding/schemes/wellcome-discovery-awards)). That second change likely closes this door for a US-based entity — a concrete deadline to check against your own structure.
- **Schmidt Sciences** — now issuing a steady stream of open RFPs, particularly in AI safety and AI-for-science, with reported grant ranges around $1M–$5M for specific 2026 calls (third-party aggregator sourcing; verify against primary RFP text). AI2050 runs at $125M over five years. **Among the most actively open, thesis-driven funders in the current cycle and worth systematic monitoring.**
- **Open Philanthropy / Coefficient Giving** — has an explicit "Apply for Funding" page and publishes its grantmaking process in detail ([Coefficient Giving](https://coefficientgiving.org/our-approach/grantmaking-process/)). Most large grants still originate from program-officer sourcing, but the process transparency makes it the best available model of how a sophisticated funder actually decides.
- **Templeton** — uses "Online Funding Inquiry" windows rather than a strict LOI funnel; grants typically up to 3 years. Co-funding from other sources strengthens applications, especially for renewals ([Templeton](https://www.templeton.org/grants/grant-faq)).
- **Sloan Foundation** — runs an "Open Source in Science" program and an OSPO institutional-support LOI call ([Sloan](https://sloan.org/programs/digital-technology/open-source-in-science)). Another direct fit for PL's open infrastructure work.
- **Arnold Ventures** — hybrid: structured RFPs alongside invitation-only relationship grants.

**Invitation or nomination only (relationship required):**

- **Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation** — "we do not accept unsolicited grant proposals," though a ~100-word inquiry may go to inquiry@moore.org. Cumulative grants approved exceed $5.97B. Internal "Four Filters" framework: importance, durability, measurability, portfolio fit ([Moore](https://www.moore.org/about/our-grantmaking)). **That 100-word inquiry is an underrated front door — treat it as a pitch that must work at 100 words.**
- **Kavli Foundation** — "does not accept unsolicited proposals"; opportunities are identified through staff, its President's Advisory Committee, and the Board, with occasional targeted calls. Its core vehicle is the endowed **Kavli Institute**, but Kavli states it is **not currently deploying endowment funding for new research institutes** ([Kavli](https://www.kavlifoundation.org/funding-approach)) — so the classic entry point is effectively closed right now.
- **Simons Foundation / SFARI / Flatiron** — funds SFARI and the Simons Collaboration on the Global Brain via RFAs, but Flatiron is an internally owned and operated institute; most Simons institute money is not externally applied for.
- **Allen Institute / Paul G. Allen Frontiers Group** — Allen Discovery Centers run via **LOI-first, invitation-narrowing** competitions ([LOI FAQ](https://alleninstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ADI-LOI-FAQs_2024-1.pdf)). Frontiers Group launched with a $100M commitment.
- **Packard Fellowships** — $875,000 over 5 years to roughly 18–20 fellows/year, nominated by ~50 invited institutions (two nominees each). Nomination-only ([Packard](https://www.packard.org/approach/fellowships-for-science-engineering/)).
- **Searle Scholars, Klingenstein-Simons, Burroughs Wellcome Career Awards, Rita Allen, Beckman Young Investigators, Pew Biomedical** — all institution-nominated. A structural barrier for a non-university research org whose staff lack an eligible nominating affiliation. **This is an argument for formal academic affiliations as a funding-access strategy**, not just a scientific one.

### 1.2 Neuroscience-specific funders

- **Brain & Behavior Research Foundation (NARSAD) Young Investigator** — **$35,000/year for 2 years**, framed explicitly as seed funding to catalyze larger grants. 2026 window ran 4 Feb–4 Mar ([BBRF](https://bbrfoundation.org/grants-prizes/bbrf-young-investigator-grants)). Small relative to your scale, but a genuine open door for individual researchers on your team and a credentialing step.
- **McKnight Endowment Fund for Neuroscience** — Scholar Award and Neurobiology of Brain Disorders Award, both nomination/institution-mediated ([McKnight](https://www.mcknight.org/programs/the-mcknight-endowment-fund-for-neuroscience/)). Commonly cited amounts around $75K–$100K/year for 3 years are unverified against the foundation's own current pages.
- **Wu Tsai Neurosciences Institute (Stanford)** and the **Wu Tsai Human Performance Alliance** — donor-endowed institute model running internal "Big Ideas" initiatives and co-funded partnership grants (e.g. with Stanford HAI). **Relevant as a collaboration/co-funding partner, not a grantor.**
- **Chen Institute, Zuckerman Institute, Dana Foundation, Focused Ultrasound Foundation, Brain Research Foundation, Kavli neuro institutes** — mostly institute/endowment or nomination models. Realistic path in is conference and program-officer relationship-building, not cold application. ### 1.3 The new institutional forms — and why they matter most to you

This is the category where PL R&D's fit is strongest, because these organizations were *built* to fund exactly the thing that neither academia nor VC will fund.

- **Convergent Research / Focused Research Organizations.** Convergent identifies scientific and infrastructure bottlenecks ("the trunk of the tech tree"), matches funders to founding teams, and launches time-bound (typically 5-year), milestone-driven nonprofit "startups" ([Convergent](https://www.convergentresearch.org/)). **Ken Griffin and Eric Schmidt jointly committed $50M to seed the model in 2023** ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/alexknapp/2023/03/17/why-billionaires-ken-griffin-and-eric-schmidt-are-spending-50-million-on-a-new-kind-of-scientific-research/)). Launched FROs include **E11 Bio** (whole-brain connectomics), **Cultivarium**, **Forest Neurotech**, and **[c]Worthy**. Note that **two of the flagship FROs are neuro** — this is directly your field. Convergent does not generally fund unsolicited outside proposals; its pipeline runs through internally sourced landscape reports identifying a gap, then matching a team. **The entry point is therefore to co-author or seed the landscape analysis, not to apply.**
- **Astera Institute** (Jed McCaleb and Seemay Chou, founded 2020). Funds "high-leverage neglected ideas that have no clear home in existing markets, institutions, or funding systems," and runs in-house teams for its biggest bets ([Astera](https://astera.org/vision/)). Its **Residency Program** is one of the most concretely specified vehicles in the entire landscape: **$125,000–$250,000/year salary plus $0–$1.5M project budgets, 12–18 months**, focused on neuroscience/AI, AI-enabled life sciences, deep tech, and open science, with a strong open-science output requirement, running roughly two cycles a year ([Astera Residency](https://astera.org/residency/)). Astera recently launched **Radial**, a life-sciences division. **This is the single closest structural analog to what PL R&D does, and the residency is a directly appliable vehicle.**
- **Renaissance Philanthropy.** Explicitly reframes philanthropy on a VC LP/GP structure: philanthropists and foundations act as **Limited Partners** funding "time-bound, thesis-driven" funds; a domain-expert **Field Strategist** acts as **General Partner** sourcing and scaling initiatives; an **Anchor Donor** underwrites each fund ([the fund model](https://www.renaissancephilanthropy.org/the-fund-model)). Reported to have mobilized **$533M** toward this model ([release](https://natlawreview.com/press-releases/renaissance-philanthropy-mobilised-533m-towards-new-model-science-and)). **The natural pitch here is not a grant application — it is proposing yourself as the Field Strategist/GP of a neurotech or open-science fund.** That is a completely different conversation, and one where your actual comparative advantage (thesis formation, network, program design) is the product.
- **Speculative Technologies** (Ben Reinhardt). A 501(c)(3) targeting "big-if-true" materials and manufacturing technologies too speculative for VC and too coordination-heavy for academia; organizes around **program leads with a 5-year vision** rather than open proposals ([model](https://spec.tech/model)). Same lesson: propose to lead or co-design a program.
- **Arc Institute, Arcadia Science** — donor-endowed institute models with internal investigator programs and (in Arc's case) external "Ignite Awards" ([Arc](https://arcinstitute.org/programs/core-investigators)). Peers and potential collaborators rather than funders.
- **Metascience shops** — Institute for Progress, Good Science Project (Stuart Buck), FAS's Metascience initiative. **These are policy organizations, not grantors**, but they are high-value allies: they shape how NIH/NSF allocate, they convene the funders, and co-authored work with them is a credible route into funder attention. Buck's manifesto cites that **42–45% of scientists' time on federally funded research goes to administration**, and that the share of federal research grants to younger investigators has fallen more than ten-fold since 1980 ([Good Science Manifesto](https://goodscience.substack.com/p/a-good-science-manifesto)). Those are the numbers the new funders are reacting against; framing your model as a structural correction to them is the argument that lands.
- **Prize, retroactive, and quadratic funding.** XPRIZE-style competitions (the Wildfire purse is $11M) reward already-built solutions rather than funding upfront R&D — a capstone target, not a funding source. **Optimism RetroPGF, Gitcoin quadratic funding, and Protocol Guild** distribute retroactively or via matching pools. Your own prior conclusion — route crowd participation through existing retroactive/quadratic infrastructure rather than building small-donor programs — is well supported by the cost analysis: small-donor programs consume the scarce resource (senior FTE-hours) at the worst ratio of any channel.

## 2. Segmentation and targeting

### 2.1 The five-axis segmentation that actually predicts fit

Unlike LP targeting, where check-size arithmetic dominates, grant targeting is dominated by **access mode and thesis fit**. The axes that matter:

1. **Access mode** — open call / LOI-gated / nomination-only / invitation-only / relationship-only / co-design (propose to lead a program). This is the *first* field, because it determines the entire play. A nomination-only funder is not "hard," it is a different game with a different first move (get an eligible institution to nominate).
2. **Instrument** — grant / fellowship / program funding / institute endowment / prize / milestone contract / OT / recoverable grant / PRI. The same funder often has several, with different doors.
3. **Thesis proximity** — how close is their stated interest to your program, on a 0–5 scale? And critically: *has their thesis moved recently?* New program launches are the single best-timed entry point.
4. **Eligibility constraints** — entity type (501(c)(3) required? for-profit acceptable? foreign entity acceptable?), geography, PI affiliation requirement, indirect-cost policy. These are hard gates.
5. **Decision architecture** — program-officer-driven / board-driven / principal-driven / peer-review panel. This determines *who you actually need to persuade* and on what timescale.

### 2.2 The prospect research layer

Standard practice, and it is mostly mechanical enough to automate:

- **Form 990-PF filings** for private foundations — grant histories, grant sizes, trustee lists, and the actual distribution of what they fund versus what they say they fund. Candid's Foundation Directory aggregates ~300,000 grantmaker profiles tied to 990 data.
- **Recent grantee cohorts** — the strongest signal of current thesis. A funder's last two years of grants tell you more than their mission statement.
- **Program officer identification and beat mapping** — who owns which portfolio, how long they have been there, what they funded before, what they publish and where they speak.
- **RFA/NOFO monitoring** — Instrumentl (400,000+ funders, ~15,000 active RFPs), GrantStation, agency feeds, ARIA's Substack, Schmidt Sciences' RFP page. Note **GrantHub sunsets 31 January 2026**, with users migrating to Instrumentl.
- **Board and trustee overlap** — the warm-path graph. Foundation boards are small and heavily overlapping with the tech and science worlds you already inhabit.

### 2.3 The scoring model

**Hard gates:**
- Entity eligibility (tax status, geography, PI affiliation)
- Indirect-cost policy compatible with your cost structure (Keck's zero-indirects rule is a real disqualifier for some work)
- Program area within scope
- Cycle open or predictably opening

**Weighted score:**
- Thesis proximity (weight 3)
- Access path quality — existing relationship / warm intro to a program officer / board overlap / cold (weight 3)
- Award size relative to your program's need (weight 2)
- Duration — multi-year unrestricted scores far higher than one-year restricted, because of the FTE-hour cost of renewal (weight 3)
- Strategic value — does this funder unlock others, or signal to peers? (weight 2)
- **Total cost to pursue in senior FTE-hours** (weight 3, *negative*)

That last term is the one most organizations omit, and it is the one your own analysis says matters most. **A $200K restricted one-year grant that costs 120 senior hours to win and 60 to report is a net loss for PL R&D.** Encoding cost-to-pursue as a first-class negative weight would change the ranking of most prospect lists dramatically, and is probably the single most valuable non-obvious feature of the tool.

## 3. The relationship engine

### 3.1 How a sophisticated funder actually decides

Coefficient Giving's published process is the best available model, and is worth treating as a template for how *any* program-officer-driven funder likely operates ([Coefficient Giving](https://coefficientgiving.org/our-approach/grantmaking-process/)):

1. **Strong Interest.** An internal investigator identifies a potential grantee and writes a short internal memo to sync with senior staff *before* investing more time — explicitly to use everyone's time efficiently.
2. **Investigation.** 1–3 months for standard grants, **4–6 months for complex or large commitments**. Deep diligence and relationship-building.
3. **Conditional Approval.** A detailed proposal goes to leadership, decided within 1–2 weeks. If leadership wants substantial revisions, **the investigator loops the grantee back in.**
4. **Formal Approval.** External funding partners (e.g. Silicon Valley Community Foundation, Good Ventures) do final sign-off. "Nearly every grant that reaches this stage is approved and paid out."
5. **Evaluation and Close.** Periodic check-ins, renewal investigations before term end.

Three operational implications:

- **The real decision is made at stage 1.** Your entire top-of-funnel strategy should be aimed at getting a program officer to write that internal memo. Everything downstream is execution.
- **Stage 3 is collaborative by design.** Expect to revise with the officer. Treating the submitted proposal as final and defending it is a misread of the process.
- **Payment lag is real.** Coefficient Giving publishes median times from grants-team handoff to payment: **27 days for US nonprofits, 42 days for US universities, 61 days for international universities.** Build that into cash-flow planning; "approved" is not "banked."

### 3.2 What makes a program officer take a risk on you

Bridgespan's framework — the most articulate public account — gives four principles sophisticated funders use: **lead with organizational assets** rather than defensive risk-minimization; **resist searching for the perfect grantee**; **actively manage the diligence "tax"** on grantee time; and **reframe risk**, weighing the *impact risk of not funding* against financial, legal, and reputational risk of funding ([Bridgespan](https://www.bridgespan.org/insights/philanthropic-sourcing-diligence-and-decision-making-an-equity-oriented-approach)).

The practical translation: **a program officer needs a defensible narrative to carry into their own leadership.** They are spending their internal credibility. The best thing you can give them is a case that is easy to champion — clear, falsifiable, milestone-structured, with the obvious objections already answered. A proposal that leaves the officer to invent the answers is asking them to take your risk *and* do your work.

### 3.3 The ARPA program-manager pattern

Ben Reinhardt's "Why does DARPA work?" is the clearest public account of how PM-driven funding actually operates, and it generalizes across ARPA-H, ARIA, and the new philanthropic program offices ([Reinhardt](https://blog.benjaminreinhardt.com/wddw)):

- PMs **proactively build communities before a solicitation exists** — small workshops of people working on adjacent ideas, to figure out "which possibilities are not impossible."
- **"Performer Days"** — small private conferences for everyone working on pieces of a program, to force cross-pollination and let performers build independent relationships.
- PMs network deliberately across academia, startups, and large companies to smooth technology transition.
- **Small "seedling" grants ($50K–$1M)** validate concepts before a full program is committed.
- The best PMs can "get into the trees and still see the forest," are low-ego and genuinely curious, and resist both capture by eloquent argument and reflexive dismissal of contrarian ideas.

**The single most actionable inference:** the community-building that precedes a program is the game. If you are in the room when a PM or program officer is figuring out what the program should be, you are not competing for the award — you helped define it. This is precisely what LabWeek, Funding the Commons, and your podcast already do, and it is currently a fundraising asset you are not explicitly monetizing.

Agencies formalize the same thing as **Industry Days and Proposers' Days** ([ARPA-H](https://arpa-h.gov/news-and-events/arpa-h-101-preparing-proposers-day)), and **most awards go to teams assembled before the BAA drops**, not formed reactively after ([ARPA-H teaming](https://arpa-h.gov/news-and-events/arpa-h-101-assembling-research-team)). Attending a Proposers' Day to find teammates is table stakes; attending the pre-solicitation workshop is the actual advantage.

### 3.4 The cultivation clock

Practitioner sources converge on ranges rather than fixed numbers, and the variance is driven almost entirely by pre-existing trust:

- **Six-figure gifts:** roughly 12 months with a substantive move monthly; other practitioners report 2–3 years with 5–6 substantive moves ([Philanthropy Works](https://www.philanthropyworks.org/how-long-major-gift)).
- **Seven-figure gifts:** reported outcomes ranging from *two hours* (a warm, pre-existing relationship) to 18–30 months. The bimodality is the point.
- **Eight-figure and up:** no reliable sourced benchmark was found. The commonly repeated "3–5+ years" claim could not be confirmed. Directionally, large gifts scale cultivation time upward *but are disproportionately driven by a handful of relationship-based closes that happen fast.*

**The strategic read:** because mega-gift timing is bimodal, the highest-value activity is not shortening the long path — it is **increasing the number of principals with whom you have genuine pre-existing trust**, since those are the relationships that close in weeks. That is, again, an argument for convening.

**Total elapsed time from first contact to first dollar:** realistically **9–24 months** for relationship-based science philanthropy including cultivation; **4–12 months** from submission to award for open-call government mechanisms; ARPA-style OT programs can move in 3–6 months once a PM is engaged pre-submission.

## 4. The materials stack

### 4.1 Artifacts and what makes each best-in-class

| Artifact | Job | What makes it good |
|---|---|---|
| **One-page concept note** | Earn a meeting | Problem, technical thesis, why now, why this team, ask range. It must survive being forwarded without you in the room. |
| **Letter of Inquiry (LOI)** | Earn an invited proposal | Candid's guidance: **no more than three pages**, and notes it "can be more difficult to write than a full proposal" because of the compression ([Candid](https://learning.candid.org/resources/knowledge-base/letters-of-inquiry/)). Structure: exec summary (org, ask amount, project), org description, statement of need, methodology (activities, key staff, outcomes), other funding being pursued, closing. **At many foundations the LOI alone decides.** |
| **Full proposal** | Win | Candid's base structure is exec summary / needs statement / project description / budget. **For technical R&D, add explicit milestones with go/no-go criteria** rather than the vaguer "activities and outcomes" framing typical of social-sector proposals. Funders now calibrated on ARPA-style rigor (Schmidt Sciences, Coefficient Giving, Astera) read milestone structure as a seriousness signal. |
| **Budget + indirect-cost narrative** | Survive scrutiny | State the negotiated rate and cite the NICRA explicitly if you have one; it materially reduces scrutiny versus an ad hoc overhead percentage. |
| **Logic model / theory of change** | Satisfy process-oriented funders | **Often the wrong artifact for frontier R&D.** A strict logic model undersells technical uncertainty and reads as naive to funders who understand R&D risk. Best-in-class technical orgs substitute a **milestone/decision-tree roadmap**. Know which funder wants which. |
| **Letters of support** | Third-party validation | Weight is proportional to the signer's credibility *with that specific funder*, not general prestige. |
| **Case for support** | Be forwarded | The core narrative document: mission, urgent problem, track record, specific ask, recognition. **Write it to be forwarded**, because the program officer or donor advisor uses it to build their own internal case. |
| **Named-gift / recognition menu** | Convert capacity into commitment | Tiered dollar amounts mapped to recognition. For a science R&D org these are usually *programmatic* (a named research program, fellowship, or facility) rather than physical — a meaningful difference from a university capital campaign. |
| **Donor prospectus** | Close a mega-gift | Bespoke, often confidential, prepared jointly with the donor's advisors. Not templated. |
| **Grant report** | Fund the renewal | Bridgespan's guidance: negotiate the **reporting format and cadence as part of the grant agreement**, not after. Best-in-class funders now want mutually agreed cadences rather than boilerplate annuals. |
| **Renewal case** | Compound | Must show evidence against the *original* milestones plus a credible account of what changed and why continuation compounds rather than merely continues. |

### 4.2 How this differs from an LP deck

This is worth stating explicitly because you will be running both stacks in parallel:

- **An LP deck sells a return distribution.** One document, largely reused across a fundraise, aimed at an allocator whose programmatic engagement is minimal and whose decision criterion is risk-adjusted financial return.
- **A grant stack sells a mission-aligned bet.** It is **layered and iteratively co-authored** (concept note → LOI → invited proposal → the officer's internal board memo), shaped jointly with the funder's staff before final submission. Closer to a co-written government proposal than a sales pitch.
- **The audiences stack.** A grant proposal must satisfy the program officer *and* their leadership *and*, increasingly for R&D, technical reviewers. An LP deck must satisfy one investment committee.
- **The unit of persuasion differs.** LPs are persuaded by evidence of *edge*. Funders are persuaded by evidence of *importance plus tractability plus neglectedness* — the reason the same underlying program needs genuinely different framing, not just a different cover slide.

## 5. Process mechanics and benchmarks

### 5.1 The pipeline

The canonical cycle — **identification → qualification → cultivation → solicitation → stewardship** — with benchmarks from AFP's 2026 guidance ([AFP](https://careers.afpglobal.org/career-resources/finding-talent-8/performance-metrics-for-fundraising-teams-2026-46)):

- **Portfolio size per major-gift officer:** 100–150 prospects
- **Solicitations per year:** 40–60 for a ~125-prospect portfolio
- **Healthy portfolio movement:** 30–50% of prospects advance at least one stage annually
- **Qualification SLA:** qualify or disqualify within 90–120 days
- **Pipeline depth:** **8–12 qualified prospects per closed major gift**
- **Close rate on qualified, solicited asks:** **50–65%**

Note the reconciliation: 8–12:1 on raw qualified prospects but 50–65% on *solicited* asks, because most disqualification happens before the ask. That gap is the entire value of good qualification discipline.

**Stage weights for forecasting:** identified 10%, qualified 25%, cultivating 40%, solicited 60%, verbally committed 90%. Sum weighted, never at face value.

**Donor retention** is measured by the AFP/Growth in Giving **Fundraising Effectiveness Project**. Figures vary by measurement window — roughly **43% overall annual retention and under 20% for new donors** in AFP's guide, versus **~18% on a rolling year-over-year basis** in the Q1 2025 FEP release, which also showed total dollars rising ~3.6% while small-donor counts declined ([AFP FEP](https://afpglobal.org/news/fundraising-effectiveness-project-data-q1-2025-shows-increases-dollars-raised-declining)). Read the methodology before quoting a single number. The directional fact — **dollars up, donors down, concentration rising** — mirrors exactly what is happening in venture LP markets.

**Context:** Giving USA 2026 confirms total US charitable giving **surpassed $600 billion for the first time** ([Indiana University](https://philanthropy.indianapolis.iu.edu/news-events/news/_news/2026/giving-usa-report-2026.html)). The detailed sector breakdown is paywalled and unverified here.

### 5.2 The gift range chart

The standard tool for translating a top-line target into a required prospect list. Mechanics per multiple consultancies:

- **Lead-gift ratio ~4:1** — four to five qualified prospects per top-tier gift secured
- **"Rule of three"** — at least three qualified prospects per gift needed at every level
- **80/20** — roughly 80% of dollars from the top ~20% of donors

A worked $1M example builds a nine-tier pyramid (one $200K, two $100K, four $50K, tapering) requiring **201 total gifts sourced from 606 prospects** ([Kindsight](https://kindsight.io/resources/blog/gift-range-chart-guide/); [Funraise calculator](https://www.funraise.org/tools/gift-range-chart-calculator)). The "rule of thirds" (top 10 gifts ≈ one third, next ~100 ≈ one third, everyone else ≈ one third) is widely used in practitioner literature; its precise origin is unverified.

**Applied to PL R&D:** if the realistic answer is that senior FTE-hours are the constraint, the gift range chart argues hard for **a compressed pyramid** — fewer, larger, longer gifts. A $20M program funded by three funders at $6–7M over five years costs a fraction of the senior hours of the same $20M assembled from forty grants, and produces vastly better planning stability. **Design the pyramid around hour-cost, not just dollar-coverage.**

### 5.3 Metrics and CRM — and the gap

What a well-run development function tracks: pipeline value by stage with weighted forecast; officer activity metrics against the 40–60 solicitation benchmark; portfolio movement rate; qualification cycle time; close rate on solicited asks; retention/renewal. **Cost-to-raise-a-dollar and donor lifetime value benchmarks could not be sourced to an authoritative current figure** — worth commissioning your own internal number rather than relying on a published benchmark.

**Tooling.** Donor CRMs: Blackbaud Raiser's Edge NXT (fits orgs up to ~50,000 records already in the Blackbaud ecosystem); Salesforce NPSP (free, open source, good to ~20,000 donors) and Nonprofit Cloud (larger, more customizable, steeper setup); Bloomerang and Virtuous in the mid-market. Grant sourcing and pipeline: Instrumentl, Candid Foundation Directory, GrantStation, Fluxx (more grantmaker-side).

**The gap, stated plainly:** none of these tools are built around program-officer relationship tracking against milestone-based technical deliverables, multi-instrument capital stacks (grants + OTs + DAF gifts + PRIs + mega-donor commitments in one pipeline), or NICRA/indirect-cost compliance. They are built for annual-fund and foundation-grant workflows at a university or human-services nonprofit. **A frontier R&D org's actual requirement — unifying philanthropic pipeline, government non-dilutive pipeline, and technical milestone tracking in one system — is not served out of the box by anything on the market.** Most such orgs either bolt milestone tracking onto Salesforce or run a separate program-management system alongside a conventional donor CRM.

That is a real product gap, and combined with the LP-side gap in Report 1, it is the core of the opportunity.

## 6. The individual mega-donor path

### 6.1 The advisory layer

Wealthy tech donors rarely act alone. The people between you and the money:

- **Rockefeller Philanthropy Advisors** — nonprofit advisory offering "philanthropy roadmap" services to individuals and families ([RPA](https://www.rockpa.org/guide/your-philanthropy-roadmap/)).
- **Arabella Advisors** — for-profit consultancy managing pooled and fiscally-sponsored vehicles.
- **Longview Philanthropy** — explicitly free, bespoke, end-to-end advisory connecting major donors to vetted opportunities in existential-risk-adjacent science and technology: expert introductions, briefings, vetted grant opportunities, and **invite-only donor convenings**, plus pooled funds ([Longview](https://www.longview.org/about/)). **Being on a Longview-style vetted list is a high-leverage position.**
- **Founders Pledge** — targets startup founders pledging future exit proceeds. **Directly relevant to the crypto and tech-founder wealth in your network.**
- **Bridgespan** — the strategy consultancy most large foundations and mega-donors retain.
- **Family office staff** — often the actual gatekeeper, and frequently the person who does the diligence.

### 6.2 DAFs as the actual counterparty

Donor-advised funds at **Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, and especially Silicon Valley Community Foundation** are the dominant vehicle for large tech giving ([Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/a-short-history-of-the-fast-and-furious-rise-of-dafs/); [SSIR](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/the_charity_that_big_tech_built)). Note that Coefficient Giving's own formal approval routes grants through SVCF and Good Ventures as the legal funding parties.

**Practical consequence:** you may need to satisfy *two* entities' diligence — the advisory or sourcing organization that makes the decision, and the DAF or foundation that legally disburses. The tool needs to model these as separate nodes with separate requirements, or you will repeatedly be surprised by a second round of paperwork after a "yes."

### 6.3 The donor journey pattern

Synthesized across the documented cases, the recurring shape is:

1. **A personal thesis** about a structural gap in how science gets funded or organized — not a response to a cold pitch.
2. **An exploratory tranche**, run informally: personal checks, a small team.
3. **Institutionalization** once the model validates — a foundation, a named institute, or an incubator that can launch multiple entities.
4. **Escalating multi-tranche commitments** tied to demonstrated proof points.
5. **Delegation to professional program staff and advisors** as scale grows, converging back toward the program-officer-mediated structure in §3.1.

The **Broad Institute** is the best-documented example of stage 4: an initial founding gift enabling a decade of operation, then in 2008 an "unprecedented $400 million" endowment gift on top of $200 million already pledged (total $600 million), then a further **$100 million in 2013** to fund a second decade ([Broad](https://www.broadinstitute.org/history-leadership/founders)). Initial bet → proof of model → transformative endowment → renewal.

**Schmidt Sciences** illustrates the disclosure pattern of these vehicles: funding is announced **by initiative, not as a campaign total** — $125M over five years for AI2050, $10M for AI safety research in 2025 (27 projects, including support to Yoshua Bengio), $45M referenced for ocean biogeochemistry, unspecified but significant funding for four astronomy projects in January 2026 ([Schmidt Sciences overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmidt_Sciences)). If you are tracking them, track *program launches*, because a program launch is the moment capacity exists.

### 6.4 What this means for you specifically

Your position on this map is unusual and mostly favorable:

- **You already sit inside the network these donors inhabit.** Crypto-origin wealth, tech founder wealth, and the longevity/neuro-interested principal population overlap heavily with Protocol Labs' ecosystem. Most science R&D orgs have to build that access from zero; you have to *organize* it.
- **The podcast and the convenings are cultivation infrastructure.** A long-form neurotech and AI podcast is, mechanically, a cultivation instrument: it gives you a legitimate, non-transactional reason to spend two hours with exactly the people who fund this field, and it builds public credibility that precedes any ask. Ted Seides' Capital Allocators and Patrick O'Shaughnessy's Invest Like the Best are the canonical demonstrations of this converting into capital relationships. **Track podcast guests and gathering attendees as a formal prospect pipeline, with the explicit rule that no ask happens in the same interaction.**
- **The Field Strategist / GP framing is likely your highest-value pitch.** Renaissance Philanthropy's model, Convergent's model, Astera's residency, and Speculative Technologies' program-lead model all want the same thing: a credible domain expert with a thesis and a network, who can deploy other people's philanthropic capital well. That is a description of you. It is a materially better positioning than "here is our research program, please fund it," because it puts you on the GP side of the table in a market where the LP side is looking for GPs.

## 7. Failure modes

1. **Applying cold to invitation-only funders.** Wasted senior hours with near-zero base rate. The correct move for Moore, Kavli, Simons, and Packard-class funders is relationship construction (or the 100-word inquiry), not an application.
2. **Optimizing dollars instead of hours.** The single most important correction given your cost structure. A portfolio of small restricted grants can look like fundraising success while destroying the research capacity it was meant to fund.
3. **Using a logic model where a milestone roadmap belongs.** Reads as naive to technically sophisticated funders; the reverse error (milestone roadmap to a process-oriented social-sector funder) also fails.
4. **Treating the proposal as final.** The process is collaborative at stage 3. Defending a submitted document rather than revising it with the officer misreads the game.
5. **Ignoring the payment lag.** "Approved" is 27–61+ days from money, plus the foundation's own board calendar. Cash-flow planning that assumes otherwise creates avoidable crises.
6. **Missing the eligibility cliff.** Wellcome's geographic narrowing from 29 Oct 2026, EIC's EU-establishment requirement, ARIA's >50% UK-cost rule, NIH's foreign-component restrictions, and Keck's zero-indirects policy are all hard constraints that invalidate months of work if discovered late. **These belong in the tool as gate fields with effective dates.**
7. **Underbuilding the program-officer graph.** Program officers move. Their portfolios shift. A relationship mapped to an institution rather than a *person* decays silently.
8. **Small-donor programs.** Your own analysis is right: the cost structure is bad and the scarce resource is senior FTE-hours. Route crowd participation through existing retroactive and quadratic funding infrastructure rather than building bespoke machinery.
9. **Single-funder dependence.** The flip side of the compressed pyramid. A program with one funder at 70% has a governance and continuity risk that sophisticated funders themselves will flag in diligence. Target 3–5 anchors, not one.
10. **Failing to formalize the convening-to-capital path.** If the gatherings, podcast, and LabWeek-style events are not instrumented — who attended, what they cared about, what follow-up happened — the single most valuable asset in the system produces no compounding return.

## 8. A 12-month operating plan

**Phase 0 — Positioning (months −2 to 1)**
- Decide the **entity and instrument story**: what can PL R&D receive, from whom, in what form? 501(c)(3) status, fiscal sponsorship options, expenditure-responsibility implications for foundation grants to non-charities, and whether a dedicated vehicle is warranted. This determines eligibility for a large fraction of the landscape and should be settled first.
- Write the **case for support** and a **milestone roadmap** for each program.
- Build the prospect database: ~200 funders segmented by the five axes in §2.1, with hard gates and effective dates encoded.
- Map the **program officer graph** — people, not institutions — and the warm paths from your existing network.

**Phase 1 — The high-yield plays (months 1–6)**
Run these in parallel because they have different clocks:
- **The new-intermediary track:** Renaissance Philanthropy (Field Strategist/fund thesis), Astera (residency and direct), Convergent Research (co-author a landscape analysis in a neuro bottleneck), Speculative Technologies (program lead). This is where your comparative advantage is highest and the access mode most favorable.
- **The open-call track:** CZI EOSS and Sloan Open Source in Science for PL's open infrastructure work; Schmidt Sciences RFPs for AI-for-science; ASAP RFAs if any neuro program fits; Keck if a basic-science program fits its criteria.
- **The government track:** identify 2–3 ARPA-H / DARPA / ARIA program areas, engage PMs *before* solicitations, attend the pre-solicitation workshops and Proposers' Days, and begin teaming. Simultaneously, begin the NICRA process if you have federal award history to base it on.
- **The mega-donor track:** formalize the podcast and gathering attendee lists as a cultivation pipeline. Build relationships with 2–3 advisory intermediaries (Longview, Founders Pledge, RPA) so you appear on vetted lists.

**Phase 2 — Cultivation depth (months 6–12)**
- Move 8–12 qualified prospects toward solicitation for each major program need.
- Convert two or three principal relationships into exploratory tranches (the stage-2 pattern in §6.3) rather than pushing for the full ask.
- Establish reporting cadences with existing funders as negotiated terms.

**Throughout:** at least one high-quality convening per quarter with an explicit (internally tracked, never externally stated) funder-development objective alongside its scientific one.

## 9. What this implies for the tool

The grant side adds requirements the VC side does not have:

**Entity and access model**
- **Access mode as a first-class field** — open call / LOI-gated / nomination-only / invitation / relationship / co-design — because it determines the entire play, not just the difficulty.
- **The funder's internal process as a modeled object**: their stages, their board calendar, their docket deadlines, their typical investigation duration. You are managing a pipeline inside their pipeline.
- **Program officers as primary entities**, with tenure, portfolio, publication and speaking history, and mobility tracking. When an officer moves, every relationship mapped to them needs re-scoring.
- **Legal-entity separation**: the deciding entity (an advisory org) is often not the disbursing entity (a DAF or foundation). Model both, with separate requirement sets.

**Gates with effective dates**
- Eligibility rules change on announced dates (Wellcome's 29 Oct 2026 geographic narrowing is the live example). Gates need validity windows, and the system should surface "this door closes in N days."
- Indirect-cost policy per funder, checked against your actual cost structure.

**Cost-to-pursue as a first-class negative weight**
- Estimated senior FTE-hours to apply, to manage, and to report, per opportunity. Ranking by *expected dollars per senior hour* rather than by expected dollars. Given your stated constraint, this is arguably the most important single feature.

**Cycle and calendar intelligence**
- RFA/NOFO monitoring with auto-matching against your program profiles.
- Board and docket calendars for target foundations.
- Program-launch detection — a new program is the moment capacity exists.

**The convening-to-capital instrument**
- Attendee and guest registries linked to the prospect graph.
- Interaction logging with an explicit **"no-ask" flag** so cultivation touches are counted as cultivation rather than polluting solicitation metrics.
- Path scoring from any attendee to any target funder.

**Proposal and reuse machinery**
- A content library of reusable blocks (org description, team bios, milestone frameworks, budget narratives, past-performance) with per-funder assembly. This is where most of the FTE-hour savings actually come from.
- Version tracking of what was sent to whom, since funders talk and inconsistency is expensive.

**Compliance**
- NICRA status and rate, per-funder indirect policy, and the resulting net-to-program calculation surfaced at the point of prioritization.
- Expenditure-responsibility flags for grants from private foundations to non-charity recipients (see Report 3 for the detail).
- Reporting obligations calendar derived from grant agreements.
