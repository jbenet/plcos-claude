# Report 3 — Commonalities, Differences, and Synergies

**How VC fund fundraising and science-philanthropy fundraising relate, and how to target the overlap as one system**

*Prepared 19 September 2026. Sourced inline. The tax and legal material is general information for research purposes, not legal or tax advice; several specific IRS regulatory pages could not be independently re-verified and are flagged accordingly.*

---

## 0. The argument in one page

Four claims:

1. **The two processes are structurally isomorphic.** Both are relationship-graph search problems with long cycles, tiny conversion rates, stage-gated pipelines, coverage-ratio arithmetic, and reputational contagion across a small dense community. Roughly 70% of the machinery is shared, and **that 70% is exactly the part worth building software for.**

2. **They differ on three axes that are not reconcilable and must be modeled separately**: the objective function (return vs. impact), the persuasion unit (edge vs. importance-tractability-neglectedness), and the artifact style (one reused document vs. a layered, co-authored stack). Trying to run one process for both produces materials that fail at both.

3. **The overlap is not a niche — it is where the frontier capital actually lives.** The same principals and institutions increasingly hold an LP book and a grant book, often through multiple legal entities under one brand. CZI runs four entities. Schmidt runs at least three. Gates runs three. **The single biggest practical failure mode in raising from this population is proposing the wrong instrument to the wrong legal entity inside what looks from the outside like one relationship.**

4. **Protocol Labs is already structurally a dual-capital machine and is not yet operating as one.** A research program (PL Neuro), a fund (PLC Neurotech), a convening engine (LabWeek, Funding the Commons), a media asset (the podcast), and a public-goods funding infrastructure heritage (retroactive and quadratic funding). That stack is the thing Breakthrough Energy, Prime Coalition, and The Engine each built deliberately. The synergy is available; what is missing is the shared graph and the sequencing discipline.

## 1. Structural isomorphism — the shared 70%

| Dimension | VC LP raise | Science grant/philanthropy raise | Shared? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core problem** | Search + matching under a credibility constraint | Same | ✅ Identical |
| **Cycle length** | 12–18 mo typical, 24+ tail | 9–24 mo including cultivation | ✅ Same order |
| **Conversion** | ~300 prospects → ~50 commitments; 3–7 touches over 6–18 mo | 8–12 qualified prospects per closed major gift; 50–65% close on solicited asks | ✅ Same shape, different denominators |
| **Pipeline** | Identified → meeting → diligence → soft circle → hard circle → docs → wire | Identification → qualification → cultivation → solicitation → stewardship | ✅ Isomorphic stages |
| **Forecasting** | Coverage ratio 2–4x; probability-weighted pipeline | Gift range chart; 4:1 lead-gift ratio; stage-weighted forecast | ✅ Same math, different vocabulary |
| **Concentration** | Median 23 LPs/fund; anchor >22% of fund | 80/20 — top 20% of donors = 80% of dollars | ✅ Same power law |
| **Anchor dynamics** | 15–30% pre-committed unlocks the rest | Lead gift sets the pyramid and validates the case | ✅ Identical mechanism |
| **Warm intro dominance** | Existing LPs are the highest-conversion referral channel | Program officers and board overlap dominate access | ✅ Identical |
| **Reputational contagion** | Reference leakage; a burned list is near-irrecoverable | Program officers and foundation boards talk; a bad reference circulates | ✅ Identical |
| **Diligence** | ILPA DDQ, ODD, references from LPs/founders/co-investors | Program officer investigation, references, site visits, org due diligence | ✅ Similar depth |
| **Renewal economics** | Quarterly LP updates fund the Fund II raise | Grant reports fund the renewal | ✅ Identical logic |
| **Decision architecture** | Investment committee, consultants, board | Program officer → leadership → board/funding partner | ✅ Both have an internal champion who spends credibility |
| **Cost structure** | Senior partner hours are the constraint | Senior FTE-hours are the constraint | ✅ Identical, and both usually mismeasured |

The two markets even share the same macro pathology right now: **dollars up, counts down, concentration rising.** Venture: 101 first-time funds in 2025 (lowest since 2011), top 10 funds taking 32.9% of capital, three firms taking 48.1% of H1 2026 ([NVCA 2026 Yearbook](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nvca-releases-2026-yearbook-charts-a-venture-industry-in-transition-302740013.html); [PitchBook Q2 2026](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q2-2026-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor)). Philanthropy: total US giving passed **$600B for the first time** while donor counts declined and dollars rose on larger gifts ([Giving USA 2026 via IU](https://philanthropy.indianapolis.iu.edu/news-events/news/_news/2026/giving-usa-report-2026.html); [AFP FEP](https://afpglobal.org/news/fundraising-effectiveness-project-data-q1-2025-shows-increases-dollars-raised-declining)).

**The operational consequence of that shared pathology is the same in both cases:** stop optimizing for breadth, start optimizing for a small number of deep, high-conviction, long-duration relationships — and instrument them properly, because you cannot afford to lose one.

**The single most important shared insight:** in both domains the real decision is made by an *internal champion* spending their own credibility inside their own institution — a program officer writing a "Strong Interest" memo, or an LP partner taking a manager to their IC. Everything you produce should be optimized to make that person's internal case easy to win. That reframing collapses a large fraction of the apparent difference between the two disciplines.

## 2. Where they genuinely differ

These differences are real and must not be smoothed over by a unified tool.

### 2.1 The objective function

An LP is a fiduciary optimizing risk-adjusted return, subject to mandate constraints. A funder is optimizing mission impact, subject to legal and reputational constraints. This is not a framing difference — it inverts the meaning of the same facts. **"This will take fifteen years and may not have a commercial exit" is a defect to an LP and a feature to a funder** (it is precisely the reason no market will fund it). The same program must therefore be described with different *load-bearing* claims, not a different tone.

### 2.2 The persuasion unit

- **LPs are persuaded by edge**: differentiated access, selection, or support, evidenced by something inspectable — a facility, a media operation with measurable inbound, a research program with counted throughput, an attributed track record.
- **Funders are persuaded by importance × tractability × neglectedness**: why this matters, why it can be done, and why no one else will do it. The neglectedness argument is the one with no analog in venture — an LP does not care that nobody else is doing it, except insofar as it implies less competition for deals.

### 2.3 The artifact style

An LP deck is largely one document, reused, refined, and defended. A grant stack is **layered and co-authored**: concept note → LOI → invited proposal → the officer's internal board memo, with the funder's own staff shaping it before final submission. Treating a grant proposal like a pitch deck — polished, final, defended — is a category error, and treating an LP deck like a grant proposal — hedged, process-heavy, milestone-laden without a return case — is the reverse error.

### 2.4 Legal form and eligibility

Venture has a light constraint set (accredited investor status, the 100/250 LP cap, concentration limits in LP mandates). Philanthropy has a heavy one: entity type, geography, PI affiliation, indirect-cost policy, expenditure responsibility, self-dealing, excess business holdings. **Grant eligibility is frequently a hard binary that invalidates months of effort if discovered late**, and it changes on announced dates. There is no venture equivalent of "this funder stops being able to fund you on 29 October 2026."

### 2.5 What "no" means

In venture, a pass is usually final for that vintage but leaves the relationship intact for the next fund. In philanthropy, a pass often means "not this cycle, not this framing" and the relationship is the asset that persists — funders frequently fund on the second or third framing of the same idea. **The re-engagement logic is therefore different, and a tool that models both needs different decay curves.**

### 2.6 Timeline predictability

Grant cycles are calendared: deadlines, dockets, board meetings, review timelines (NSF targets ~6 months to decision; EIC Step 1 takes 4–6 weeks; Coefficient Giving's investigation is 1–3 months standard, 4–6 for large). LP decisions are not calendared — they happen when conviction crosses a threshold. **The grant side is forecastable by calendar; the LP side is forecastable only by probability weighting.** A unified pipeline view must handle both.

## 3. The overlap — who funds both

This is the strategically important section, because the overlap population is growing and is the natural home for a neurotech thesis.

### 3.1 The patterns

Three distinct structures, which require three different approaches:

**Pattern A — One entity, two checkbooks.** The **Gates Foundation's Strategic Investment Fund** (formerly "Program-Related Investments," rebranded 2018) makes equity, debt, and guarantee investments across ~90+ companies, with an investment committee working alongside program teams, explicitly to "further the foundation's programmatic goals, not to generate income," recycling proceeds back into the grant budget ([Gates SIF FAQ](https://sif.gatesfoundation.org/faq/)). The investment and grant sides sit under one roof with coordinated decisioning. **Heron Foundation** is the extreme version: it eliminated the separate PRI bucket entirely and moved 100% of its endowment to mission-aligned investing by December 2016 ([Heron](https://www.heron.org/enterprise/)).

**Pattern B — Sister entities, common control.** **CZI** runs four vehicles: CZI LLC (investments), the CZI Foundation (a 501(c)(3)), a donor-advised fund at Silicon Valley Community Foundation, and CZI Advocacy (a 501(c)(4)) — one brand, four wrappers, so each type of check has a compliant home ([CZI](https://chanzuckerberg.com/grants-ventures/)). **Emerson Collective** is an LLC family office investing across stages (Commonwealth Fusion, Boom, OpenAI, Anthropic) while spinning out independent nonprofits (XQ Institute, the $3.5B Waverley Street Foundation, Yosemite as a cancer venture fund) and holding media assets. **Schmidt**: Schmidt Sciences (nonprofit, launched 2024), the former Schmidt Futures, and Hillspire (family office investing) — coordination between them is not publicly documented. **Bezos**: Bezos Earth Fund ($10B committed, ~$2.4B granted across 371+ grants, explicitly exploring "hybrid-financing models") alongside Bezos Expeditions for direct equity ([Bezos Earth Fund](https://www.bezosearthfund.org/)).

**Pattern C — Capital + independent brain.** **Good Ventures** (Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna's 501(c)(3), founded 2011) holds the capital but has essentially no program staff, outsourcing recommendations to **Coefficient Giving** (renamed from Open Philanthropy in November 2025), which is now a multi-donor advisory directing well over $200M from donors beyond Good Ventures, with separate governance and overlapping board seats ([GiveWell on the relationship](https://www.givewell.org/about/gw-op-relationship)). **This is itself a template worth noting** — an independent thesis-driven grantmaking brain paired with a capital-holding foundation is structurally close to a GP/LP relationship, and is exactly what Renaissance Philanthropy has formalized.

### 3.2 The named map (for the targeting database)

**Foundations with both endowment/MRI and grant programs:** Gates (SIF + grants), Wellcome (£37.6bn endowment with a documented appetite for venture, plus >£1bn/yr in grants — [PEI](https://www.privateequityinternational.com/wellcome-trusts-love-for-venture/)), Ford ($1B MRI commitment + $350M PRI revolving fund + grants — [Ford](https://www.fordfoundation.org/news-and-stories/news-and-press/news/ford-foundation-commits-1-billion-from-endowment-to-mission-related-investments/)), MacArthur (Catalytic Capital Consortium anchor), Rockefeller (Zero Gap Fund), Kresge ($350M+ Social Investment Practice), Heron (100% mission-aligned), Omidyar Network (501(c)(3) + LLC dual structure), Builders Vision, Grantham.

**LLC/hybrid vehicles:** CZI, Emerson Collective, Arnold Ventures (converted from foundation to LLC in January 2019, reported $4.77B in nonprofit-side assets as of 2024), Ballmer Group, Pivotal Ventures, Omidyar Network ([Inside Philanthropy](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/home/these-6-major-funders-are-llcs-not-foundations)).

**Individual principals running both sides:** Gates (personal $2B into Breakthrough Energy Ventures + Gates Ventures + the foundation), Jed McCaleb (Astera + Navigation Fund, the latter scaling from $4M/36 grantees to >$60M/217 grantees by 2025 — [Navigation](https://www.navigation.org/about)), Patrick and John Collison (Arc Institute + individual investing), Ken Griffin (Griffin Catalyst, >$2.5B committed, plus the Convergent Research seed), Eric and Wendy Schmidt, Sergey Brin (Catalyst4, ~$2B+ committed), Reid Hoffman (Greylock + personal science bets, e.g. Sanmai), Vinod Khosla, Laurene Powell Jobs.

**The critical modeling point:** for each of these, the *decision-maker*, the *legal entity*, the *instrument*, and the *diligence requirements* differ by which side of the house you are talking to — and frequently the two sides do not talk to each other. Treating "Schmidt" or "CZI" as one prospect is the error.

### 3.3 Why the LLC trend matters to you

LLC-structured philanthropy buys four things a private foundation cannot: unlimited direct equity investment without PRI qualification hurdles; unlimited lobbying and political spending; **no mandatory 5% annual payout** (the IRC §4942 minimum distribution rule that binds private foundations); and essentially no public disclosure — no Form 990-PF, no public grants list unless voluntarily published. The costs: ordinary tax on investment gains, and no upfront charitable deduction for the donor.

**For a fundraiser this cuts both ways.** Upside: an LLC can write a grant, a SAFE, or an equity check from the same relationship, which is enormously flexible if you have both a fund and a research program. Downside: **opacity and strategy volatility.** Inside Philanthropy's assessment is blunt — it is "difficult even to begin to estimate an LLC's assets," and CZI's pivot away from education and criminal-justice work reportedly left grantees "holding an empty bag." Some LLCs (Ballmer Group, Arnold Ventures) publish detailed grant databases; others are effectively unresearchable from public filings.

**Practical rule:** with an LLC funder, ask early and explicitly which entity will write the check and under which instrument, because it determines the paperwork, the timeline, and the diligence. And do not build a program's survival on an LLC's single-strategy commitment without a written multi-year agreement.

## 4. The instruments that bridge the two sides

This is the mechanical heart of the synergy. There is a real spectrum between a grant and an LP commitment, and most organizations use two points on it when they could use six.

### 4.1 PRIs — the primary bridge

**Program-related investments** under **IRC §4944(c)** must satisfy three tests: (1) the primary purpose is to accomplish an exempt purpose of the foundation; (2) production of income or appreciation is **not a significant purpose**; (3) no lobbying or campaign intervention. Incidental profit does not disqualify a PRI as long as a profit-maximizing investor would not make the same investment on the same terms. PRIs can be loans, equity, guarantees, or credit enhancements — and the IRS explicitly notes that accepting an equity position alongside a loan does not by itself disqualify a PRI ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations/program-related-investments)).

**The reason foundations bother:** because PRIs serve a charitable purpose, they generally **count toward the mandatory 5% annual minimum distribution**. A PRI check into a fund or company can simultaneously satisfy payout obligations *and* plant an equity or loan position that may recycle. That is a genuinely attractive proposition for a foundation, and it is the argument you make to them.

**How a PRI becomes an LP stake.** In practice, three forms:
- **A subordinated or first-loss tranche** in a blended fund, where the foundation's capital absorbs early losses so market-rate LPs sit senior.
- **A guarantee** against downside, which requires no cash outlay unless triggered (MacArthur/C3's $25M guarantee for the SDG Loan Fund is the model).
- **A concessionary LP commitment** — capped at return of principal, or accepting a below-market preferred return — into a fund whose structure and reporting satisfy §4944.

**Mission-related investments (MRIs)** are different: not a distinct IRS statutory category, but endowment-side, market-rate-seeking investments made under ordinary prudent-investor standards, chosen for values alignment without needing to meet the concessionary §4944 test. Ford makes the split explicit: MRIs seek risk-adjusted market rate and go through a board-level MRI Committee; PRIs may be concessionary and fund "catalytic, innovative opportunities" ([Ford FAQ](https://www.fordfoundation.org/work/challenging-inequality/mission-investments/frequently-asked-questions/)).

**Reporting burden:** PRIs require documented pre-commitment analysis showing the three-part test is met, ongoing monitoring that the charitable purpose continues, and Form 990-PF disclosure. This is real friction for the foundation — which means **a fund that has already done the structural work to be PRI-eligible, and can hand a foundation a ready-made memo supporting the §4944 analysis, has a material competitive advantage.** That is a concrete, buildable asset.

### 4.2 The canonical working example: Prime Coalition / Azolla

Worth studying closely because it is the closest structural template for a neurotech fund:

- **Prime Coalition** is the 501(c)(3), which "tightly tethers" its nonprofit charitable purpose to the for-profit fund's corporate form.
- **Azolla Ventures** is the affiliated **for-profit fund manager** running day-to-day investment operations; it raised a **$239M blended fund** combining philanthropic (PRI/DAF/foundation) capital with concessionary and market-rate tranches ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/07/13/azolla-ventures-new-fund/); [Prime Coalition](https://www.primecoalition.org/press/azolla-ventures-launch-press-release)).
- Since 2014 Prime reports **mobilizing $315M+ from 247 capital partners into 58 companies** ([Prime Coalition](https://www.primecoalition.org/investments)).
- **Trellis Climate** is its impact-first catalytic program pairing risk-tolerant capital with nonprofit sector-transformation work for first-of-a-kind infrastructure.
- The stated operating principle is **"one relationship, many instruments"** — a single donor/LP relationship can flow into a PRI, a DAF grant, or an LP commitment, with nonprofit staff managing the relationship regardless of instrument.

**That last sentence is the design brief for your tool.**

## 5. The unified targeting model

### 5.1 One graph, many instruments

The core architectural claim: **the durable object is the relationship with a funder family; the fund commitment, the grant, the co-investment, and the advisory role are all events on that relationship.** Most tooling inverts this — it makes the transaction primary and the relationship a derived attribute — and that inversion is precisely why nothing on the market handles the overlap.

The model:

```
FunderFamily (the principal, the institution, the brand)
  └── LegalEntity (private foundation | public charity | LLC | 501(c)(4)
                   | DAF account | family office | fund vehicle | operating co)
        ├── taxStatus, disclosureRegime, payoutObligation
        ├── permittedInstruments[]
        ├── mandateConstraints[] (with effective dates)
        └── decisionArchitecture (who decides, what calendar)
  └── Person (principal | program officer | investment staff | advisor
              | family office staff | board member | gatekeeper)
        ├── portfolio / beat
        ├── tenure, mobility history
        └── relationshipStrength, lastTouch, trustLevel
  └── CapitalFlowEdge (the instrument)
        ├── type: grant | PRI | MRI | DAF distribution | LP commitment
        |         | direct equity | SAFE | recoverable grant | guarantee
        |         | first-loss tranche | royalty/milestone | OT | prize
        ├── fromEntity, toEntity, amount, dates
        ├── complianceRegime (expenditure responsibility? §4944 memo? UBTI?)
        └── reportingObligations[]
  └── RelationshipEdge (non-capital)
        ├── type: advisor | board member | co-investor | referrer
        |         | convening attendee | podcast guest | scientific collaborator
        └── startDate, endDate  ← because these roles SEQUENCE
```

**Two design points that fall out of the research:**

- **Time-bound edges are essential.** The overlap is usually *sequenced*, not simultaneous: a grant funds early research, equity follows once a company forms; or a principal is a podcast guest, then an advisor, then an LP. A model without edge start/end dates cannot represent the actual pattern.
- **People are primary, not institutions.** Program officers move. Investment staff move. Principals delegate. Your existing instinct — the PL-network-tuned append-only event log with people as primary entities — is the correct foundation, and it happens to be exactly what this domain needs.

### 5.2 The unified scoring model

Prospects should be scored for **the best instrument**, not just for fit. A single scoring pass should return something like: *"Foundation X: grant fit 4/5 via program officer Y (invitation-only, next docket March); PRI fit 3/5 into Fund II (they have a PRI program, $2–5M typical); MRI fit 1/5 (endowment mandate excludes first-time managers). Recommended play: grant first, PRI at Fund II."*

That recommendation — **which instrument, in which order** — is the actual output that neither a VC CRM nor a donor CRM can produce, and it is the product's central claim.

Shared scoring dimensions:
- Thesis/mission proximity
- Relationship depth and warm-path quality
- Decision speed and architecture
- Capacity (check size range per instrument)
- Signal value to other funders
- **Cost-to-pursue in senior FTE-hours** (negative weight)
- **Instrument flexibility** — how many of your vehicles can they fund? (This is the overlap-specific dimension, and high scores here are disproportionately valuable because one relationship-building investment amortizes across multiple asks.)

### 5.3 Sequencing playbooks

The research supports a few repeatable sequences:

**Grant → LP.** A funder makes a grant to the research program, observes execution against milestones for 12–24 months, then commits to the fund — often via PRI, because the PRI counts toward payout and preserves upside. This is the strongest sequence because the grant *is* the diligence. The Engine's relationship with MIT and Harvard has this shape; Prime Coalition's relationships routinely do.

**LP → grant.** An LP in the fund sees the research program producing the fund's deal flow and funds the program to protect and expand its own pipeline. Rarer, but very strong when it happens, because the LP has a direct economic reason to fund the upstream.

**Convening → relationship → either.** The attendee becomes a known quantity, then a collaborator, then a funder of whichever vehicle fits. This is the highest-volume top-of-funnel available to you and the least instrumented.

**Program officer → principal.** For family-office and LLC funders, the staff relationship often precedes access to the principal. Treat the staff relationship as the objective, not the obstacle.

**The rule that governs all of them: never run two sequences at the same funder simultaneously without disclosure.** See §7.

### 5.4 A practical prioritization heuristic

For each prospect, compute **expected dollars per senior FTE-hour, summed across instruments, discounted by probability and time.** Rank by that. It will produce a list that looks nothing like a conventional prospect list — it will concentrate hard on a small number of multi-instrument, high-trust, long-duration relationships, and it will ruthlessly deprioritize the small restricted grants and small LP checks that consume the most hours per dollar.

Given that senior FTE-hours are your stated binding constraint on the R&D side and partner hours are the constraint on the fund side, this is the correct objective function for both, and it is the same one. That is the strongest single argument that these two functions should share a system rather than sit in separate tools.

## 6. Compliance — what the tool must encode

*General information, not legal or tax advice. Several IRS pages could not be independently re-verified in this research; the framework descriptions below reflect well-established statutory structure and should be confirmed with counsel before being relied upon.*

| Rule | What it constrains | Flag the tool needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Accredited investor status (Reg D, Rule 501)** | Whether an *entity* (not just the principal) can take an equity/SAFE/LP position | Per-legal-entity accreditation flag. Large foundations and family offices qualify easily; a small or newly formed grant vehicle may not. |
| **IRC §4944(c) — PRIs** | Three-part test: primary charitable purpose; income/appreciation not a significant purpose; no lobbying | Per-fund "PRI-eligible?" flag plus a stored supporting memo. Counts toward the foundation's 5% payout. |
| **IRC §4942 — minimum distribution** | Private foundations must distribute ~5% annually; LLCs need not | Payout-obligation flag per entity. Drives *timing* — a foundation with unmet payout late in its fiscal year is a motivated buyer. |
| **IRC §4945 — expenditure responsibility** | A private foundation granting to a non-public-charity (a for-profit, an LLC, a foreign org without equivalency determination) must do a pre-grant inquiry, use a written restricted-use agreement requiring segregation and accounting, and collect annual reports it then reports to the IRS | Flag on every foundation→non-charity edge. **This is the pathway that lets foundations fund for-profit science ventures or FRO spinouts directly by grant**, so it is an enabler, not just a burden — but it adds weeks and paperwork. |
| **IRC §4941 — self-dealing** | A foundation cannot transact to primarily benefit a disqualified person (its officers, directors, substantial contributors, or entities they control) | **Disqualified-persons list per foundation entity**, auto-checked against every proposed grantee/investee. This is the flag most likely to matter to you personally, given that you sit across a fund, a research program, and a network. |
| **IRC §4943 — excess business holdings** | A foundation's holdings plus those of all disqualified persons generally cannot exceed ~20% of a business enterprise | Concentration check on any large equity or LP position. Matters if a foundation or family vehicle wants an outsized stake. |
| **UBTI (IRC §511–514)** | Tax-exempt LPs can generate unrelated business taxable income from leverage or active-business income | Per-fund-vehicle flag: does the structure avoid debt-financed income, or is a blocker required? |
| **ECI and blocker structures** | Foreign and tax-exempt investors often route through an offshore blocker to convert effectively connected income into blocker-level dividends | Per-vehicle "blocker available?" flag. **Ask this before pitching a foreign sovereign or a tax-exempt LP**, because the answer determines whether they can invest at all. |
| **Grant eligibility with effective dates** | Geography, entity type, PI affiliation, indirect-cost policy — and these change | Gate fields with validity windows and "closes in N days" alerts (Wellcome's 29 Oct 2026 narrowing is the live case). |
| **MFN / side-letter state** | An LP term granted once may propagate | Structured register of terms granted, carve-outs, and open elections. |

**The single sentence to carry from this section:** the biggest practical failure mode in raising from the overlap is proposing the wrong instrument to the wrong legal entity inside a funder family that, from the outside, looks like one relationship — and that is a data-modeling problem with a data-modeling solution.

## 7. Failure modes specific to running both

1. **Pitching the same idea to both sides of one funder without disclosure.** Approaching a foundation's program officer for a grant while separately approaching its investment team for a PRI, without either knowing, damages credibility with both — even, and especially, when they sit in the same building. **Disclose the full picture and let them route it.** Sophisticated funders find this impressive, not confusing, when it is presented deliberately.
2. **Shopping the same IP twice.** Presenting a nonprofit research project and a for-profit spinout of the identical IP to overlapping funder networks without a clear firewall invites scrutiny you do not want.
3. **Self-dealing exposure.** If a foundation's program team grants to a research group and its investment arm then takes equity in a for-profit spinout of that same work, the foundation needs a documented process — an information barrier or at minimum board-level disclosure. **Symmetrically, you need an internal allocation and conflicts policy governing how PL Neuro's output reaches PLC Neurotech.** This is the thing an institutional LP and a sophisticated foundation will both ask about, and having it written before anyone asks is worth a great deal.
4. **Grant funds building value later captured by insiders.** The structural concern behind §4941. Address it explicitly: who owns IP developed under grant funding, what happens on spinout, and what the fund's terms are when it invests in a spinout from a program it helped fund.
5. **Voice incoherence across the stack.** Three audiences — founders, LPs, funders — and three registers. The failure is not having three registers; it is having three *different stories*. USV's solution generalizes: one intellectually honest public thesis, with the harder quantified material in the appropriate private channel.
6. **Cannibalizing attention.** Both raises consume the same scarce senior hours, and both are 12–24 month campaigns. Running them fully in parallel at full intensity is not feasible for a small team. **Sequence them deliberately, or explicitly staff them separately** — which is one argument for the PL Neuro lead hire carrying fundraising responsibility.
7. **Over-indexing on the overlap.** Not every funder is dual-capable, and forcing a dual pitch on a single-instrument funder wastes the meeting. The overlap is a high-value segment, not the whole market.
8. **Assuming the two sides of a funder talk to each other.** They usually do not. A warm relationship on the grant side does not automatically open the investment side, and sometimes the investment side has an explicit policy against funding grantees.

## 8. The Protocol Labs case

You have, in one network, the components that Breakthrough Energy, Prime Coalition, and The Engine each assembled deliberately:

| Component | The analog | Your asset |
|---|---|---|
| Upstream research that originates the science | The Engine's MIT pipeline; Convergent's FROs | **PL Neuro**, PL R&D |
| A fund that capitalizes what comes out | BEV; Azolla; Engine Ventures | **PLC Neurotech Fund I** |
| A convening engine that builds the relationship graph | Foresight's Vision Weekends; Capital Allocators; Edge City | **LabWeek, Funding the Commons, your gatherings** |
| A media asset that compounds credibility | a16z's media op; Colossus; Not Boring | **The neurotech/AI podcast** |
| Public-goods funding infrastructure | Gitcoin; Optimism RetroPGF | **PL's retroactive/quadratic funding heritage** |
| An operator-technologist principal with domain credibility | Katie Rae; Sam Altman; Jed McCaleb | **You** |

What is missing is not a component. It is **the shared graph and the sequencing discipline** — and that is what the tool is for.

Three specific strategic moves that the research supports:

**A. Design PLC Neurotech Fund II (or a parallel sleeve) to be PRI-eligible from the start.** Azolla is the proof that a blended vehicle can combine foundation PRI capital, DAF capital, and market-rate LPs in one fund. For neurotech — long duration, thin exit comparables, high societal value — this is arguably a *better* fit than it is for climate. It unlocks an entire LP class that is currently structurally closed to you, and it turns the duration problem from a liability into the reason a foundation should participate.

**B. Explore a royalty/milestone structure for PL Neuro.** The Cystic Fibrosis Foundation's $3.3B Kalydeco royalty sale is the single most consequential precedent in disease-area philanthropy. A research program that funds development in exchange for royalty rights can eventually fund itself, which changes the pitch to every funder: you are not asking them to fund a permanent cost center, you are asking them to seed a self-sustaining engine. Neurotech's long clinical timelines make this harder than in CF, but the structure is worth serious modeling.

**C. Pitch the GP role, not the grant.** Renaissance Philanthropy's LP/GP model, Convergent's FRO model, Astera's residency, and Speculative Technologies' program-lead model all want the same input: a credible domain expert with a thesis, a network, and the ability to deploy philanthropic capital well. Approaching them as an applicant is a weaker position than approaching them as a prospective Field Strategist or program lead for a neurotech thesis. **This also solves the FTE-hour problem**, because being the GP of a philanthropic fund is a fundamentally better hours-to-dollars ratio than applying for grants.

One coherent story ties all three together: *Protocol Labs originates frontier neurotech through PL Neuro, capitalizes it through PLC Neurotech, and convenes the field through LabWeek and Funding the Commons. A funder can enter at any of the three points, with the instrument that fits their constraints, and the other two compound their contribution.* That is the sentence the tool exists to make operationally true.

## 9. Tool specification — the consolidated view

Pulling together the requirements from all three reports.

### 9.1 Core data model

- **FunderFamily → LegalEntity → Person**, three levels, with entity-level tax status, mandate constraints, disclosure regime, payout obligation, permitted instruments, and decision architecture.
- **Time-bounded edges** for every relationship and capital flow, because sequencing is the norm.
- **Append-only event log**, replayable — which you have already designed for the agentic VC architecture. The grant side needs exactly the same substrate.
- **Instrument as a first-class type** with its own compliance regime, reporting obligations, and diligence requirements.

### 9.2 Targeting and prioritization

- Hard gates with **effective dates** (eligibility, check size, mandate, conflict, cycle status).
- Multi-instrument scoring that returns **a recommended instrument and sequence**, not just a fit score.
- **Cost-to-pursue in senior FTE-hours** as a first-class negative weight; rank by expected dollars per senior hour.
- Tiering with explainable component scores and an audit trail of manual overrides.

### 9.3 Pipeline and forecasting

- Two stage models (LP and grant) that roll up into **one coverage view** against a total capital plan across all vehicles.
- Probability weights per stage, including the soft-circle/diligence non-monotonicity on the LP side.
- Calendar-driven forecasting on the grant side (dockets, deadlines, review windows) alongside probability-driven forecasting on the LP side.
- Decay rules, re-engagement triggers, and **different decay curves for the two domains** (a grant "no" decays slower than an LP "no").

### 9.4 The relationship graph

- Warm-path computation with path-quality scoring.
- **Convening and media instrumentation**: attendee registries, podcast guest lists, session co-hosts — linked to the prospect graph, with a **no-ask flag** so cultivation is counted as cultivation.
- Referral attribution, so the graph learns which paths actually convert.
- Program officer mobility tracking, with automatic re-scoring when someone moves.

### 9.5 Materials and knowledge

- Versioned content library with per-recipient assembly and delivery tracking.
- DDQ and proposal answer libraries with reuse — **the largest single source of FTE-hour savings.**
- **Objection ontology** — a structured taxonomy rather than free text, so clustering surfaces "three LPs raised duration this week" as a materials problem rather than three separate conversations.

### 9.6 Compliance

- The full §6 flag set, checked automatically at the point of prioritization rather than at the point of closing.
- MFN/side-letter register as structured, queryable data.
- Reporting-obligation calendar derived from executed agreements.
- Internal allocation-and-conflicts policy encoded as checks on PL Neuro → PLC Neurotech flows.

### 9.7 The learning loop

This is where your backtest-replay instinct pays off. With an append-only event log across both domains you can ask: which sequencing actually worked? which objections predicted a pass? which convenings produced capital, at what lag? which warm-path types converted? **No existing tool in either market can answer these, because none of them holds the whole graph.** That is the defensible core.
