# Report 5 — Broad Marketing, Content, and Reputation as a Capital Engine

**Channel strategy, compounding assets, segment efficacy, cost, and measurement — for PLC Funds and PL's R&D programmes**

*Prepared 19 September 2026. Sourced inline; vendor-published and single-source figures are flagged. The securities-law material is general information, not legal advice.*

---

## 0. The argument in one page

1. **None of this closes your EOY raise, and you should design it knowing that.** Content's time-to-effect is 6–24 months. Everything in this report is a 2027–2029 asset. The correct posture for the next 14 weeks is: keep publishing what you already publish, instrument it properly so you can measure it later, and put zero new senior hours into launching new channels. The compounding starts when you start, but it does not pay this quarter.

2. **The legal architecture comes first.** Under Rule 506(b) you cannot generally solicit. The workable and widely used structure is two-tier: **firm-level thesis, research, and brand content published openly; anything identifying a specific fund, its terms, its performance, or an active raise kept strictly relationship-gated.** Every example of great VC content marketing — USV's theses, Bessemer's Cloud reports, a16z's media operation, Lux's letters — sits on the open tier and carefully doesn't cross the line. Draw your line explicitly, write it down, and put it in the publishing checklist.

3. **The highest-return asset available to you is the one nobody else in neurotech can build: the canonical primer.** Stratechery's "aggregation theory," Convergent Research's public definition of the Focused Research Organization, Bessemer's State of the Cloud — these work because they became the shared vocabulary of a field, so every subsequent conversation implicitly re-cites the author. Neurotech is at exactly the stage where its vocabulary is still being set. That window closes.

4. **Convening is the highest-conversion channel for the audiences you need, and you already run it.** Foresight's Vision Weekends, Edge City residencies, LabWeek, Funding the Commons. The mechanism is relationship density: convening compresses a multi-year trust cycle into days, and produces a verifiable graph. Almost nobody instruments it. You should.

5. **Measurement in this domain is genuinely hard and mostly honest approximation.** Long cycles, dark social, and self-report bias mean clean attribution is unavailable. What a small team can actually run: branded search volume, self-reported attribution accepted with known bias, inbound quality, and annual cohort analysis by first-known-touchpoint. Anything more elaborate is theatre.

## 1. Channel-by-channel

### Long-form written — essays, theses, annual letters, research reports, newsletters

**Reaches:** self-selecting, high-intent readers — allocators, scientists, senior founders. Never a mass audience, which is the point.
**Time-to-effect:** slow. 6–24 months to build a readership that matters; individual pieces can spike overnight.
**Cost:** low cash, **high senior time**, and essentially undelegatable if the value is your specific judgment. Fred Wilson's AVC has run daily since 2003 ([avc.com](https://avc.com/2018/04/usv-thesis-3-0/)).
**Durability:** very high. Essays get cited for years.
**Measurability:** moderate — subscriber counts are legible, attribution to capital is not.

**Verdict for you:** the single best fit for your comparative advantage. You think in systems and write like an engineer; that is exactly the register that moves technical allocators and scientists, and it is the register that produces a primer (§4).

### Podcasts — hosting vs guesting

**Hosting** is a multi-year infrastructure investment that builds a proprietary distribution channel and, crucially, a **Rolodex-as-content-asset**: guest booking *is* relationship sourcing. **Guesting** is cheap and fast — one appearance on Invest Like the Best or Capital Allocators reaches thousands of allocators at near-zero production cost, but the reputational asset accrues to the host.
**Time-to-effect:** hosting 1–3 years; guesting can show inbound within weeks.
**Cost:** $2,000–5,000/month for a lean host-led show up to $25,000+/month fully produced ([Castos](https://castos.com/podcast-cost/); [talks.co](https://talks.co/p/podcast-production-costs/)), plus 3–6 senior hours per episode.
**Measurability:** downloads are a widely criticized vanity metric in B2B ([b2b-better](https://b2b-better.com/blog/why-b2b-podcast-downloads-dont-matter-2026/)). **Who listens matters, not how many.**

**Verdict for you:** you already run the show, which means the marginal cost of instrumenting it properly is near zero and the marginal return is high. Specifically: **track every guest as a relationship record**, and treat booking as a deliberate act of graph construction rather than editorial calendar filling. The mechanism that made Capital Allocators and Invest Like the Best valuable to their hosts is that 90 minutes of thoughtful conversation builds rapport that would otherwise take years of private meetings — and produces a public track record of your judgment that prospective LPs can audit before ever meeting you.

Add: **guest more.** A neurotech GP appearing on allocator-facing shows is a cheap, fast, high-leverage move, and it is on the open tier of the 506(b) line as long as you talk thesis and not fund terms.

### Video / YouTube

Good for founder-facing reach and for "showing the work" in a science context — lab tours, demo days, technical explainers. Weak for allocators, who read rather than watch. Highest production cost per unit of the owned channels. Evergreen explainers compound; commentary decays.

**Verdict:** low priority, with one exception — a well-made visual explainer of *how a BCI actually works* is exactly the kind of artifact that becomes a default reference (§4), and neurotech is unusually well-suited to visual explanation.

### Social — X vs LinkedIn

These reach genuinely different audiences and the split is contested in 2026. Practitioner pieces argue "VC Twitter is moving to LinkedIn" as X's algorithm and audience shift toward general content ([everywhere.vc](https://ideas.everywhere.vc/p/vc-twitter-is-moving-to-linkedinheres)); others still find X better for real-time founder/builder discourse ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/whats-better-x-twitter-or-linkedin/)).

The practical read: **X still concentrates technical founders, scientists, and crypto/AI builders. LinkedIn better reaches family-office principals, institutional allocators, and corporate/foundation staff** who use it as professional identity infrastructure and don't otherwise engage in public tech discourse.

Cost: near-zero cash, moderate senior time, not outsourceable without losing authenticity. Durability: low — posts decay in days; only cumulative following compounds.

**Verdict:** your natural home is X and it reaches the wrong half of your LP audience. LinkedIn is worth deliberate, low-frequency use — a post per substantive publication, written for allocators rather than builders. **And this is the channel where the 506(b) line is easiest to cross accidentally.** No "we're raising" posts.

### Events — self-hosted vs sponsoring vs speaking

**Self-hosted** is highest cost, highest control, highest compounding: it creates a proprietary community graph and a calendar anchor. **Sponsoring** buys adjacency fast and builds no proprietary asset. **Speaking** is cheap for individual reputation and builds no organizational infrastructure.

Self-hosted flagship events run well into six figures in direct costs, and the senior-time cost is very high — but concentrated in **curating the invite list, which is the actual value driver, not the production values.**

**Verdict:** your strongest existing asset. See §3.

### Webinars, seminars, AMAs

Efficient for audiences already in the pipeline — existing LPs, portfolio operator communities. Low reach outside them. **Best used as mid-funnel nurture, not awareness.** For a 506(b) fund, an invitation-only LP webinar on thesis developments is a legitimate and underused relationship-maintenance tool.

### PR and earned media

Moves peer perception faster than owned content but is transient unless it's a profile that becomes a durable reference. For the science side, **Nature, Science, The Transmitter, and STAT carry outsized credibility with scientists, program officers, and foundation staff precisely because they are perceived as independent of the funder** — a favourable feature there is worth more per unit of effort than an equivalent amount of owned content for that audience.

Cost: boutique tech/science PR retainers typically $8,000–25,000/month (**unverified** — consistent with market norms but not independently confirmed). Measurability: poor; share-of-voice and sentiment are approximations.

**Verdict:** not a priority for the fund. **Is a priority for PL Neuro**, where third-party scientific validation is a load-bearing credibility asset with foundation program officers, and where you already have a relationship with at least one journalist covering the field.

### Open source and open data

Costless to distribute, high credibility with exactly the audience that discounts marketing claims and respects working code. Durability is very high — a widely adopted library or dataset generates citations, stars, and inbound technical talent for years with near-zero ongoing spend. Measurement is comparatively good (stars, forks, citations, downloads); conversion to capital is indirect, running through recruiting and technical credibility rather than LP dollars.

**Verdict:** this is PL's native language, and it is genuinely differentiating in neurotech, where most of the field is closed. An open neuro dataset or tool released under the PL banner would do three things at once: establish technical credibility with scientists, create a grant-fundable artifact (CZI EOSS and Sloan's Open Source in Science both fund exactly this — see Report 2), and generate the kind of inspectable proof-of-edge that LPs credit (Report 1, §3.3).

### Academic and preprint publication

For the R&D org, the most durable reputation asset available: cited for decades, intrinsically credible, and the only channel that properly reaches the scientist segment. Slowest time-to-effect of anything here; strongest compounding.

## 2. Best-in-class examples, and the actual mechanism

Studying these is only useful if you extract the *mechanism*, so each entry below names it.

**a16z's "New Media" operation.** A dedicated in-house team plus forward-deployed operators, explicitly modelled on "CAA for tech," shipping shows and newsletters roughly 5x/week ([a16z](https://a16z.com/what-is-new-media/); [2026 update](https://a16z.com/what-is-new-media-in-2026/)). **Mechanism: marketing-as-fund-differentiator, not marketing-as-LP-raise.** Portfolio companies get launch packages and channel access, which makes a16z more attractive to founders choosing among term sheets in a commoditized capital market. The LP benefit is second-order — it's evidence of sourcing edge, not a direct solicitation.

**USV's blog and published theses.** AVC daily since 2003; version-numbered theses ([usv.com/writing](https://www.usv.com/writing/)). **Mechanism: filtering.** By publishing exactly what they will and won't back, USV pre-qualifies inbound — founders self-select in or out, saving partner screening time. The LP benefit is thesis clarity observable over fifteen years of public updating, which substitutes for a track record no first-time fund has.

**Not Boring → Not Boring Capital.** Packy McCormick built a large newsletter audience with frequent, accessible essays on frontier-tech companies, then converted it directly into a fund ([case study](https://selffundedbychrissie.substack.com/p/how-packy-mccormick-turned-not-boring)). **Mechanism: audience-to-LP conversion.** Readers who trusted his judgment enough to give him capital. This is the clearest media-to-fund case in venture — though exact subscriber-to-LP conversion numbers were never disclosed, so treat the ratio as **unverified**.

**Colossus / Invest Like the Best, and Capital Allocators.** Podcast-first media whose primary audience is other allocators ([Colossus](https://colossus.com/series/invest-like-the-best/); [Capital Allocators](https://www.capitalallocators.com/)). **Mechanism: relationship pre-selling at scale.** Hosting a guest for 60–90 minutes builds rapport that would take years of private meetings, and produces a public record of thoughtful judgment that converts into co-investment access and LP relationships. **This is the mechanism your podcast already runs — the gap is instrumentation, not strategy.**

**Stratechery.** ~40,000+ paid subscribers at roughly $12/month, an estimated $5M+/year, built over a decade from a 2013 launch ([analysis](https://blockbuster.thoughtleader.school/p/how-ben-thompson-got-40000-paid-newsletter); [about](https://stratechery.com/about/)). **Mechanism: the primer.** "Aggregation theory" became the industry's shared vocabulary, so every subsequent conversation about platform strategy implicitly re-cites Thompson. See §4.

**Bessemer's State of the Cloud / Cloud Index.** An annual, heavily benchmarked report ([bvp.com/atlas](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/state-of-the-cloud-2024)). **Mechanism: category ownership through genuine utility.** Because it's real benchmarking data rather than opinion, it earns re-citation every year, and each edition inherits authority from the last. a16z's State of Crypto, Coatue's EMW deck ([coatue.com](https://www.coatue.com/blog/company-update/coatues-2025-emw-conference)), and ARK's Big Ideas all run the identical play: **a dated, designed, annual artifact becomes a calendar event that press covers predictably.**

**Lux Capital's LP letters.** Written for existing LPs, quoted publicly ([example](https://www.luxcapital.com/content/lux-lp-letter-2024q2)). **Mechanism: thought-leadership-by-candor.** A private document that builds public reputation for judgment among a far broader allocator audience than the recipient list. Note the 506(b) care required: letters discussing macro judgment are open-tier; letters discussing fund performance are not.

**First Round Review and YC's essay corpus.** **Mechanism: founder pre-education.** Useful, non-promotional tactical writing builds trust before any pitch relationship exists, lowering the cost of winning competitive deals.

**Science-side examples — the most relevant set for PL.** Asimov Press partnered with Astera Institute on a two-year collaboration on "how science is done," using magazine-quality science writing to build the funder's reputation ([note](https://substack.com/@asimovpress/note/c-100756302)). Works in Progress (under Stripe) and Asterisk function as house journals giving their funding ecosystems an intellectual identity that attracts aligned donors, staff, and grantees more efficiently than direct solicitation. **Convergent Research's public writing on the FRO model** ([FAS](https://fas.org/publication/focused-research-organizations-a-new-model-for-scientific-research/); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2025/former-mit-researchers-advance-new-model-innovation-0606)) is the template case: **by naming and defining a new organizational category in public, Convergent became the default reference and partner whenever anyone discusses FROs.** Nadia Asparouhova built individual-analyst authority on open source and tech philanthropy without running an institution at all.

**Convening: Foresight's Vision Weekend** ([foresight.org](https://foresight.org/engage/vision-weekend/)), Edge City and Zuzalu-style residencies ([Gitcoin case study](https://gitcoin.co/case-studies/zuzalu-and-pop-up-cities-temporary-coordination-experiments)), and **Protocol Labs' own LabWeek and Funding the Commons** ([protocol.ai](https://www.protocol.ai/blog/labweek-public-goods)). **Mechanism: relationship density and serendipity that no remote content replicates**, converting into co-investment, co-founding, and grant relationships that persist afterwards — and positioning the host as the connective infrastructure of a field rather than a participant in it.

## 3. Efficacy by audience segment

The evidence base here is narrative and case-study, not statistically rigorous; quantified conversion-to-capital data is proprietary and largely unpublished. Treat the table as informed judgment, not measurement.

| Segment | What actually reaches them | What doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| **Institutional allocators / FoFs** | Long-form research, podcast guesting on allocator-specific shows, peer referral, ILPA-adjacent forums | Social media; consumer-style brand marketing |
| **Family office principals** | Relationship- and event-driven. Annual letters, invitation-only salons, personal introduction | Broad content; almost anything scaled |
| **Family office CIOs/staff** | Closer to institutional allocators — research reports, industry conferences, LinkedIn consumed professionally | X/Twitter |
| **UHNW individuals** | Earned media profiles (Bloomberg/FT), events, peer word-of-mouth. Brand halo matters more than thesis granularity | Newsletters they won't subscribe to |
| **Foundation program officers** | Trade and scientific press, peer-reviewed credibility, named workshops, public frameworks (the FRO pattern) | Self-promotional content — this audience is professionally skeptical of it |
| **Government program managers** | Same as program officers, plus pre-solicitation workshops and technical publications | Marketing of any kind |
| **Scientists / researchers** | Preprints, open datasets and code, science press. Podcasts build awareness; credibility follows the technical trail | Fund-brand content |
| **Founders** | The most content-responsive segment by far — YC essays, First Round Review, Not Boring, podcast guesting demonstrably shape which VCs founders approach first | — |

**The strategic read for PLC Neurotech:** your content reaches founders and scientists well, and family-office principals poorly. Founders and scientists are your *deal flow* and your *credibility substrate*, not your capital. So content is genuinely valuable to the fund — but through the sourcing-edge channel (it makes the "we see the best neurotech deals" claim inspectable to LPs), not through the direct-solicitation channel. **Frame it to LPs that way: the content operation is evidence of edge, not a marketing spend.**

## 4. Compounding vs decaying — and the primer strategy

Compounding assets share three traits: they are **dated and versioned reference documents**; they solve a real workflow problem for the reader (a benchmark, a framework, a dataset); and they are **re-cited by others**, not merely re-read. Bessemer's Cloud Index, YC's essays, Convergent's FRO framework, open datasets, peer-reviewed papers.

Decaying assets are commentary tied to a news cycle — hot takes on a funding round, most social posts, most PR hits. Valuable for a burst of visibility, half-life measured in days.

### The primer

The highest-leverage version of compounding content is **writing the canonical explainer that becomes the default onboarding document for a field.** Stratechery's aggregation theory is the clearest case: a single framework became the shared vocabulary, so every subsequent conversation re-cites it. Convergent's FRO writing plays the same role in science philanthropy.

Two requirements for it to work:
1. **Timing.** Write it while the field is naming itself, not after. Once a vocabulary settles, the slot is taken.
2. **Genuine explanatory usefulness independent of promotional intent.** Self-serving explainers get ignored; genuinely clarifying ones get adopted.

**The neurotech opportunity is real and time-limited.** The field is at exactly the stage where its categories are unsettled: what counts as a BCI, how to think about invasive vs non-invasive, how to stage technical risk, what the regulatory ladder actually looks like, how to value a company with a 12-year horizon. Nobody has written the document that a new investor, founder, or journalist reads first. The combined disclosed BCI-sector capitalization is above $15B with capital concentrating into a small group of firms ([Neurofounders](https://www.neurofounders.co/articles/bci-enters-a-new-funding-stage)) — meaning a lot of new entrants are about to need an orientation document, and there isn't one.

**Concretely, three candidate primers, in order of leverage:**

1. **"The Neurotech Stack" / state-of-the-field annual.** A dated, versioned, benchmarked report: the technology layers, who is working on each, financing history, clinical and regulatory milestones, and honest exit analysis. Run annually. This is Bessemer's State of the Cloud applied to a field that has no equivalent. It does quadruple duty — LP credibility, founder positioning, journalist reference, and a forcing function on your own thinking.
2. **The technical-risk-gating framework.** The milestone ladder from Report 1 §3.2, written up publicly: how to stage neurotech technical risk and what each gate means for valuation. This is the document that makes *every* neurotech investor's job easier, which is exactly why it gets cited.
3. **The funding-mechanisms primer**, bridging Reports 2 and 3: how frontier neuro research actually gets funded across philanthropy, government, and venture, and which instruments fit which stage. PL has unusual standing to write this given Funding the Commons, and it is directly useful to the foundation program officers you want to reach.

**Cost:** each is a serious piece of work — 6–10 weeks of concentrated effort with research and design support, based on how comparable annual reports are produced (**FTE-hour figures unverified**; no source gave a specific number). **This is a 2027 project, not a Q4 one.** But the decision to do it should be made now, because the field-naming window is open now.

## 5. Measurement

### 5.1 Why it's hard

Relationship cycles here run 6 months to several years from first exposure to committed capital, across many touchpoints — a podcast heard 18 months before a warm intro, a conference conversation two years before a close. **Last-touch attribution is close to meaningless in this environment.**

The **dark social / dark funnel problem** compounds it: buying committees research in channels no analytics tool observes — private chats, forwarded links, group Slacks ([Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/blog/marketing/marketing-strategy/dark-funnel-b2b/); [Cognism](https://www.cognism.com/blog/illuminating-the-dark-funnel-of-b2b-marketing)). Self-reported attribution systematically undercounts it, because people default to the last direct touch ("a friend introduced us") rather than the actual influence chain.

The broader B2B field has also largely soured on MQL/SQL funnel scoring as a proxy for pipeline health, arguing these stage-gates reward volume over quality and correlate poorly with closed revenue ([Clicknify](https://www.clicknify.com/blog/mql-is-dead-b2b-saas-enterprise-playbook); [Moving Parade](https://www.movingparade.com/blog/why-b2b-marketing-attribution-is-broken)). **Marketing-influenced pipeline** (any relationship touched by content at any point) is a more honest metric than **marketing-sourced pipeline** (first-touch credit) for long-cycle relationship businesses, though it's easier to inflate.

### 5.2 What a small team can actually instrument

Six things, all cheap:

1. **Self-reported attribution, accepted with its known bias.** A CRM field — "how did you first come across us" — asked in the first meeting, with a *second* question that does the real work: **"what had you read or heard before we spoke?"** That second question is what surfaces the influence chain the first one misses.
2. **Branded search volume** via Google Trends and Search Console. Cheap, directional share-of-mind proxy. Track "PLC Neurotech," "Protocol Labs neuro," and your own name as a series.
3. **UTM-tagged links** on everything you publish, feeding one dashboard.
4. **Platform-native engagement**, benchmarked rather than read absolutely. B2B email norms run roughly 20–28% open and 2.1–4.2% CTR, with financial services around 22.3% open / 3.2% CTR ([Averi](https://resources.averi.ai/benchmarks/email-newsletter-benchmarks)). Healthy subscriber growth for an established 5,000–25,000 list runs roughly 2–5%/month. **Read these as sanity checks, not targets.**
5. **Inbound quality, not volume.** The metric that matters: how many unsolicited approaches cite specific published work, and what fraction of those are qualified. A newsletter that produces three inbound family-office conversations a year is outperforming one with ten times the list and none.
6. **Annual cohort analysis by first-known-touchpoint year.** Reviewed once a year, not in real time. Of the LPs who committed this year, when did we first appear on their radar and through what? This is the only measurement here that actually answers the strategic question, and it only works if you have been recording first-touch consistently for years — **which is the argument for instrumenting now even though it pays later.**

**Explicitly not worth building:** media mix modelling, formal incrementality testing, or multi-touch attribution software. These require spend levels and data volumes a fund-scale operation doesn't have, and the output would be noise dressed as signal.

### 5.3 The convening-specific measurement

Since convening is your strongest channel and the least instrumented, it deserves its own treatment:

- **Attendee registry as a prospect record**, with role, organization, and the sessions they attended.
- **A no-ask flag** on every cultivation interaction, so convening touches are counted as relationship-building rather than polluting solicitation metrics — and so that, under 506(b), you have a clean record of relationship formation that precedes any offering conversation.
- **Lag-to-conversion tracking**: for each capital relationship, how long between first convening contact and first capital conversation. Over three years this tells you the real cycle length and therefore how far ahead to invest.
- **Second-order graph value**: who met whom at your event and what came of it. This is the thing that makes people come back, and it is invisible unless you ask.

| PR retainer | $8–25K/mo *(unverified)* | Moderate | Continuous | Low–moderate |

**The honest prioritization for your position:**

- **Now through EOY:** change nothing. Keep the podcast running, instrument it, tag everything, start recording first-touch. Zero new channels.
- **Q1 2027:** commit to one annual primer and start it. Add deliberate allocator-facing podcast guesting.
- **2027:** the primer ships; a small, invitation-only neuro salon runs alongside an existing LabWeek footprint rather than as a new standalone event.

The reason for that ordering: the primer and the convening are the two highest-compounding assets, and both require concentrated senior time you do not have in Q4.

## 7. The 506(b) line — how to draw it

*General information, not legal advice. Have counsel review the actual policy.*

Under Rule 506(b), an offering may not be generally solicited ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/exempt-offerings/private-placements-rule-506b)). The standard resolution used across the industry ([ACA Group](https://www.acaglobal.com/industry-insights/changing-compliance-landscape-venture-capital-other-private-markets-fund-managers/); [TheCorporateCounsel.net](https://www.thecorporatecounsel.net/blog/2024/10/a-perennial-concern-for-vc-fundraising-avoiding-general-solicitation-problems.html)) is a two-tier content strategy:

**Open tier — publish freely:**
- Thesis, market analysis, technical explainers, research reports
- Podcast episodes about the field
- Firm-level brand and hiring content
- Portfolio company news (the company's news, not the fund's performance)
- Convening and event content

**Gated tier — relationship-only, never public:**
- Anything naming a specific fund's terms, size, or economics
- Fund performance, returns, marks, or track record
- Any statement that a fund is currently raising, or has capacity
- The LP deck, PPM, data room, subscription documents
- Close dates and remaining allocation

**The practical mechanism:** a one-page written policy, plus a two-question checklist in the publishing workflow — *does this name a fund or its terms?* and *does this say or imply we are raising?* If either is yes, it goes to the gated tier. Given that you publish frequently and across multiple people, making this a mechanical check rather than a judgment call each time is the thing that actually prevents the mistake.

**One specific trap:** a podcast episode where a guest asks "are you raising right now?" and you answer on the record. Decide in advance how you answer that, and tell your producer.

## 8. What this implies for the tool

**Content as a first-class object:**
- Every published artifact as a record: type, date, URL, author, tier (open/gated), topics.
- Per-prospect delivery and engagement tracking — who received what version of what, and who opened it.
- Link between content and relationship: which prospects have read which pieces, used to prime introductions (Report 6, §5).

**Attribution capture:**
- First-touch field, captured at first contact and never overwritten.
- "What had you read or heard before we spoke" as a structured field with a topic taxonomy.
- Channel tags that survive across years, so cohort analysis works.

**Convening instrumentation:**
- Event → attendee → prospect linkage.
- No-ask flag on cultivation interactions, doubling as a 506(b) relationship-formation record.
- Lag-to-conversion computation from first convening contact.
- Second-order connection logging (who met whom).

**Compliance:**
- Tier field on every content artifact, enforced.
- Publishing checklist as a gate, not a guideline.
- Audit trail of what was published publicly and when, in case anyone ever asks whether fund material crossed the line.

**Measurement dashboard — six numbers, reviewed monthly, and no more:**
1. Branded search volume trend
2. Inbound approaches citing specific published work
3. Newsletter/podcast growth against benchmark
4. Convening attendees converted to prospect records
5. Marketing-influenced share of active pipeline
6. Median lag from first touch to first capital conversation (annual)
